# Everything Is Copywriting: Why You Need to Hire a Head of Copy

I'm the head of copy for a portfolio of 8-figure companies. I write hundreds of ads every month. And the most common complaint I hear from business owners is, "Our targeting is off."
It almost never is.
If your media buyer keeps tweaking the targeting of your ads, but you're still unprofitable, you don't have a targeting issue. You have a messaging issue.
In 2026, the copy is the targeting. And it's not only your ads. Your short-form scripts, carousels, emails, landing page, webinar, and VSL are all copy too.
Everything is copywriting. Which means every business already has a head of copy. For most of them, it's the founder at 11pm, or a junior writer who's guessing.
In this essay, I want to break down what copywriting is, why it matters more in 2026 than it ever has, what good and bad copy look like, the exercises I'd do today, and why I think the next hire you make should be a head of copy.

### What is copywriting?
When I first heard of copywriting, I thought it was the little C logos you see next to trademarks.
It's not.
Copywriting is the art of transferring information from brain A to brain B in the most effective way possible. It's persuasive writing.
![copywriting definition](https://x.com/Jayyanginspires/article/2117980328195481702/media/2117980271916294145)
In business, that looks like 3 steps:
1. Pick the right product, service or offer.
2. Find the right market (the people who need it).
3. Make it easy for those people to understand why they should want it.
Most people think copywriting is only step 3: clever words and a good hook.
But the best copywriters I know aren't poets with English degrees. They're researchers with a deep understanding of psychology.
Copywriting is thinking, not writing. Bad writing is almost always the result of muddy thinking. If you skip the thinking, it's like grabbing a shovel and digging as fast as you can before you've checked whether you're in the right spot. You can dig a beautiful hole, but it doesn't matter if it's in the wrong place.
The game is won before the pen is used.

### Why is everything copywriting?
Make a list of every place your business uses words to get someone to do something.
On the organic side:
- Short-form scripts
- Carousels
- Instagram story sequences
- Tweets
- LinkedIn posts
- Email newsletters
- Email sequences
- Landing pages
- Webinars
- VSLs
On the paid side: every ad you run.
![everywhere copy lives](https://x.com/Jayyanginspires/article/2117980328195481702/media/2117980274789425152)
A short-form video is a script before it's a video. A webinar is a sales letter you say out loud, and a landing page is a sales letter with buttons.
Code is the backend of the internet. Copywriting is the front end.
And in an age where AI can build almost anything, the remaining valuable alpha is the ability to create distribution, and distribution runs on words.

### Why does copy matter more in 2026?
1. The copy is the targeting.
Before, you needed crazy targeting to figure out who you were reaching, where they lived, and what their demographics were.
Now Meta has an update called Andromeda, which is basically Meta's AI system that does a lot of the targeting for you.
It reads the text you write and the visuals and verbal stuff you include, shows the ad to the people it thinks will respond, and then, based on their impressions and engagement, sends it to more of those people or to other people.
So if your ad says "I'm looking for healthcare business owners," Meta starts sending your ad to healthcare business owners.
![Andromeda](https://x.com/Jayyanginspires/article/2117980328195481702/media/2117980277691781121)
About 70 to 80% of our spend is broad targeting, with almost no interest targeting and barely any lookalikes. The creative and the landing page tell Meta who will respond.
2. Meta wants volume.
For every $10k you spend, you want 15 to 30 creative variations.
But volume without quality is a waste of time. It's not about recording in a different background, changing the person's shirt, or flipping the camera. The copy is the targeting, and the copy is the messaging. Find a winning angle and iterate on that angle.
![creative volume](https://x.com/Jayyanginspires/article/2117980328195481702/media/2117980280611106816)
3. AI made words cheap.
Anyone can open a chat window and get an ad, an email and a landing page in under a minute.
The easier it is to use AI to write, the more alpha there is in the thinking AI can't do. AI can't sit on your sales calls. It can't uncover the hidden facts about your business that make you different. It gives you the average of everything it's read, and the average blends in.

### What does bad copy cost you?
At the start, the founder writes the ads. And they work, because they sound like the founder and they speak to the right buyer.
Then the business grows, the founder runs out of time, and they hand it to a copywriter or an agency.
The first batch is fine. But the writer doesn't know your story, customers, or how you talk, so every batch they guess a little more.
![quality drop](https://x.com/Jayyanginspires/article/2117980328195481702/media/2117980283584790528)
A few months later, the ads sound like everyone else's. And since the copy is the targeting, generic ads find generic buyers.
I hear this on sales calls every week. Here are a few lines founders have told me, word for word:
- "The ads are not doing what we need because we don't have the right ad copy... so we're just kind of stuck."
- "Before is like the founder used to write everything, but after he tried to like hire people, then the quality started to drop."
- "People could tell, no, dude, you didn't write this. And to me, it's like, dude, our brand's burning."
- "We only spent like 50k on ads for it... ironically did 20k from ads."
Most of them don't see it as a copy problem at first. They see it as a media buying, targeting, or creative problem. So they fire the media buyer, change the background of the video, try a new agency, and nothing changes.
One founder put it better than I could: "Every 30 days, if sales are stagnant, we'll try this thing, then that doesn't really make a difference. We'll try something else. And ultimately, I just keep coming back to the same thing, which is, it all comes down to copywriting."
Another founder told me he'd hired a "head of marketing" who turned out to be a media buyer with a bigger title. "Like what does that really mean? That means like copywriter and media buyer... they actually are not good at really anything other than media buying."
You don't have a media buying or targeting issue. You have a copy issue.
Pro tip: before you blame the copy, check the market and the offer. The market outweighs any piece of copy you can write. Great copy to people who don't want it loses to average copy to people who do. Check 3 layers in order: who you sell to, what you sell, and how you sell it. Copy is the last lever.
![market and offer first](https://x.com/Jayyanginspires/article/2117980328195481702/media/2117980287242297344)

### What does good copy look like?
Before I write anything for a client, I answer 6 questions. I call them the 6 Ps of copy thinking:
1. People: Who is it for?
2. Positioning: Why you over every other option?
3. Promise: Where are you taking them?
4. Proof: Why should they believe you?
5. Priority: Why now?
6. Process: How does it work, and why is it different?
![6 Ps of copy thinking](https://x.com/Jayyanginspires/article/2117980328195481702/media/2117980289918271488)
The answers come from research. Some of the best ads I've ever written came from downloading our customers' sales calls and transcribing them, then using their exact words.
Because if you can describe your customers' pain better than they can, they'll assume you have the solution. The pain is the pitch.
Then I write with the 5 Cs: clear, concise, concrete, conversational, and cadence.

### Good copy vs bad copy: micro examples
1. Write the moment, not the thesis.
Bad: "Are you broke?"
Good: "Do you turn the brightness down on your phone when you open your bank app because you don't want other people to see your balance?"
The second one is a moment your reader has lived, so they can picture it.
![moment not thesis](https://x.com/Jayyanginspires/article/2117980328195481702/media/2117980293034565632)
2. Call out who it's for.
Bad: "Business owners, check this out."
Good: "If you're a business owner doing over $1 million a year..."
Most writing yells "hey!" at everyone, which means it's addressed to no one. Since the copy is the targeting, the callout is how Meta (and the reader) knows who the ad is for.
3. Make it concrete.
Bad: "We help business owners grow."
Good: "We took a roofing company from 14 leads a month to 70 leads a month."
Clear gets understood. Concrete gets remembered.
4. Make the promise specific.
Bad: "Makes your home sparkle."
Good: "Erases stains in under 30 seconds."
5. Talk like a person.
Bad: "Please don't hesitate to reach out."
Good: "Just text me if you need anything."
6. Fix the offer, then write it.
Copy can only communicate the value your offer already has. We don't make things up.
Here's an offer I rewrote:
Before: "Give us $10,000 and we'll reach out to 5 people a month for you for a year."
After: "Give us $10,000 and we'll book you 5 qualified meetings every month for 90 days. Or we work for free until we do."
Basically the same service. But a far more valuable offer.
![offer rewrite](https://x.com/Jayyanginspires/article/2117980328195481702/media/2117980296079626241)
None of the good versions are fancier, only more specific. And you only get specific by doing the research and the thinking first.

### Why can't the founder keep writing it?
Because you can't do both.
One founder told me on a call, "I'm either locked in on content or locked in on the business. Whichever one I'm locked in goes further."
Another one said he only works about 25 hours a week, "but I spend probably 20 of those hours on content... I'm the bottleneck."
And even when you have the time, you're too close to it. As another founder told me, "You can't read the label from inside the jar."
Once you're doing over $100k a month, every hour you spend writing hooks is an hour you're not spending on the business.
So you hire a writer and hand them a doc of ideas. But you're still the bottleneck, because the writer has to come back to you for every story, number, and opinion. Or, as one founder told me, "by the time I'm reviewing it, I'm rewriting everything anyway."
![founder bottleneck](https://x.com/Jayyanginspires/article/2117980328195481702/media/2117980299187683328)
Why not just hire an agency?
A lot of agencies feel easy at the start, because they take care of everything. The downside is that eventually they pass you off to junior people, and quality goes down. Now you're stuck with them.
And they write for one channel. The ads agency writes your ads, someone else writes your emails and scripts, and nobody makes sure it all says the same thing.
One founder I talked to described exactly the role he was missing (someone to own his reels, stories, emails, webinars, and funnels) and then named it himself: "a pretty big role, like head of copy, if you will."

### What does a head of copy do?
A head of copy owns 4 jobs: positioning, messaging and strategy, the day-to-day execution, and the iteration loop.
![4 jobs of a head of copy](https://x.com/Jayyanginspires/article/2117980328195481702/media/2117980301825822720)
1. Positioning.
How your business is perceived in the customer's mind, and why you over every other option.
This is the one-liner: "For [person], we're the [category] that [unique benefit], unlike [alternative]." If a stranger can't repeat it back after hearing it once, it's too complicated. If you confuse, you lose.
2. Messaging and strategy.
What you say, to who, and in what order.
This is where the research lives. The head of copy builds the source of truth every writer works from: a Story Bible (your story, proof, and beliefs), a Why They Buy sheet (your customers' exact words for their pain, dreams, and why they bought), and a voice guide (how you sound, and the words you'd never use).
In practice, we look at who your best customers are (the ones who pay the most, stay the longest, and we enjoy serving the most). We take their exact phrasings from when they joined. Then we make sure that messaging shows up across the rest of the funnel on purpose.
For one client in trading education, the pattern between the people who bought was a phrase they kept saying: they didn't want to guess, they wanted to know. That became the North Star angle for everything: "Stop guessing."
3. Day-to-day execution.
The head of copy writes or edits the ads, scripts, emails, posts, landing pages, webinars, and VSLs, so every channel says the same thing in the format that fits it.
4. The iteration loop.
Copy is never done. The head of copy looks at what's working, figures out why, and feeds it back in.
Explore with organic, and exploit with paid. If a tweet or LinkedIn post does well, it's probably because the idea behind it did well, since there are no fancy graphics or visuals. So we validate the idea, then ask how we can say that one core idea in a thousand different ways.
On the ad side, we test a lot of angles using static images first, because then you know it's the angle and not the visuals. Then we double down on the winners with video.
![iteration loop](https://x.com/Jayyanginspires/article/2117980328195481702/media/2117980304925491200)
Which brings me to the question I'd ask every business owner reading this: If your best copywriter quit tomorrow, would your ads quit with them?
With a head of copy, the answer is no. If a writer leaves, the next one starts with everything the last one learned.

### How do you know if you need one?
Here are the signs I look for:
1. You (the founder) still write or approve every ad, email, and script.
2. Your ads plateaued, or you know they could be more profitable but you don't have time to dig into the messaging.
3. Your media buyer keeps changing the targeting, and the numbers don't move.
4. Your organic content, ads, emails, and sales calls all say slightly different messages.
5. You read your own content and cringe.
6. Every new writer takes 3 months to "get" your voice, then leaves.
![6 signs](https://x.com/Jayyanginspires/article/2117980328195481702/media/2117980307978911745)
If you checked 2 or more, copy is probably one of the cheapest growth levers left in your business.

### Exercises to do today
Even if you never hire anyone, do these 5. They're the same exercises we do in the first weeks with every client.
Exercise 1: The copy audit.
Open a Google Doc and list every place your business uses words to get someone to do something. Every ad, email, script, page, post, and slide.
Next to each one, write who wrote it and when it was last updated.
Watch for 2 patterns: how long the list is, and how many lines say "nobody" or "a year ago."
![copy audit](https://x.com/Jayyanginspires/article/2117980328195481702/media/2117980310675902464)
Exercise 2: Build a Why They Buy sheet.
Download 5 sales calls (or 10 customer reviews or DMs). Transcribe them. Then copy out every line where a customer describes their problem, what they tried before, what they want, and why they bought.
Keep their exact words, including typos, filler, and half-finished sentences. Never paraphrase.
Then turn 5 of those quotes into 5 hooks. For example, a customer saying "With my kids in school, I feel like I finally have the time to do something for myself" became "If you've been looking for a way to make money around your kids' schedule without starting a whole business, you need to hear this."
Exercise 3: The competitor claim sweep.
Open the Meta Ad Library and pull up 20 competitors. Write down the main claim behind each ad.
If 5 of them are making the same claim, your market has heard it, so stop making that claim and look for the one none of them make.
Exercise 4: Rewrite 5 claims so you can point at them.
Imagine your reader can't hear you. You can't point at "proven results." You can point at a number.
Take 5 claims from your website or ads and rewrite each one with a number, a timeframe, a name of a type of customer, or a specific outcome. If you can't, you know what proof to go get.
![point-at test](https://x.com/Jayyanginspires/article/2117980328195481702/media/2117980313486069761)
Exercise 5: Read it out loud.
Read your best ad and your landing page out loud. Fix anywhere you stumble.
Then ask: would I say this to a customer over coffee? If not, rewrite it until you would.

### How we do this for clients
We become our clients' head of copy in 4 steps:
1. Audit. We look at the positioning, the messaging, why people buy, the customer data, and every piece of copy running today. We're looking for where copy is leaking money.
2. Build the source of truth. We build the Story Bible, the Why They Buy sheet and the voice guide, so every word traces back to something true.
3. Execute. We write the ads, scripts, emails, posts, pages, and VSLs, from the founder's own words first, with an editor reviewing every draft before the founder sees it.
4. Iterate. Every week we look at what's working, and the winners feed the next batch.
This isn't a relationship where we work with you, hand you off to a junior writer, send you generic reports, and let the quality go down over time. And there's no reason you should be working with a team and still doing all the writing on top of it.
![how we do it](https://x.com/Jayyanginspires/article/2117980328195481702/media/2117980316153589760)
A few house rules we hold ourselves to:
State the facts and tell the truth. We don't write lies or manipulate the truth to sound better. If a number isn't documented, it doesn't go in the copy.
No fake urgency. If the scarcity isn't true, we don't write it.
