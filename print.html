Six P's of Copy Thinking

Jay's core copywriting framework: before writing anything, fill in six Ps.

"The first step to getting good at copywriting is to follow what I call the six Ps of copy thinking... people, positioning, promise, proof, priority and process." — course 1 (~00:03:36)

PQuestionNotes
PeopleWho is the offer for?Can't answer "why us" until you know "who is they". Worked out via empathy maps and mining sales calls.
PositioningWhy them over others?How your product is perceived in the customer's mind. Strong positioning = brand recognition + lubricates sales. Case: Claude Hopkins / Schlitz beer — won by stating the facts (4,000ft artesian wells, filters, 1,000-year-old mother yeast) when every competitor was making the same "pure beer" claim.
PromiseWhat do they actually get?Not features/deliverables — the outcome, i.e. who the customer becomes. Test: the "so what?" test (deliverable → benefit → ... → core outcome). Example: Steve Jobs' iPod pitch ("a thousand songs in your pocket").
ProofWhy should I trust you?"Trust is belief with evidence. Belief without evidence is called faith." Three types: (1) authority by association, (2) your own results, (3) client stories (strongest). Proof must match the avatar (a 50-year-old Asian woman needs proof from similar women). Exercise: the deaf-and-mute test — you can only point (logos, numbers, "$106M launch in 3 days").
PriorityWhy act now?People move toward pleasure or away from pain; "people will jog toward pleasure but sprint away from pain." Three levers: scarcity, urgency, cost of inaction (his favorite) — "deprivation is the cousin of motivation".
ProcessHow does the offer work?A good process: (1) explains why other solutions fail, (2) creates a believable path, (3) feels different, (4) is easy to understand. Find yours: "Most people believe X, it's wrong because of Y, and the answer is actually Z" — see unique mechanism.

Worked example (course 1, ~00:16:24)

Alex Hermosi:

  • People: business owners doing >$500K/yr
  • Positioning: "we teach you what's working in our portfolio companies"
  • Promise: learn the 1–3 things to scale past your current level
  • Proof: "Last year my portfolio generated over $250M in aggregate revenue"
  • Priority: if you don't act, "you don't have a business, you have a job"; workshops get booked out
  • Process: the value acceleration method (compilation of everything done across industries)

Resulting ad opens: "If your business is doing between $1–3M a year, you're in what we call the swamp — too busy to work ON the business, but not enough cash to hire. That's the vicious cycle of key-man risk..."

Empathy Map & Sales-Call Mining

The "People" P is built with an empathy map (course 1, ~00:05:06).

How to draw the empathy map

Two axes:

  1. Current reality — what the avatar is doing/seeing/hearing/smelling/feeling day-to-day. Golden rule: treat people how you want to be treated; but the platinum rule is better: "treat other people the way they want to be treated" — put yourself in their shoes, not them in yours.
  2. Future — down = fears ("this is hell: where do they not want to end up?"); up = goals ("this is heaven").

He won't start writing until he has "an overabundance of material" — more context than he knows what to do with.

Pro tip: talk to the customers (course 1, ~00:06:28)

"The best way to research who your avatar is is to talk to your customers. Some of the best ads we've ever run is when we downloaded all of our sales calls, uploaded into AI, transcribed, identified the specific reasons why they bought, and said those exact things back to them."

Example transformations (sales-call phrase → ad hook):

What the customer said on the callAd hook written from it
"I'm ready to build something that gives me more freedom, flexibility and control over my time and income""The most dangerous thing a woman can do is become disgustingly financially independent."
"I'm excited to invest in myself... escaping the 9-to-5 to have more freedom and time to live""The normal path will never make you a millionaire nowadays."
"I've been a stay-at-home mom for years... I finally have time to do something for myself""If you've been looking for a way to make money around your kid's schedule without starting a whole business, you need to hear this."

The core principle

"If you can describe someone's pain point better than they can, they'll subconsciously believe that you have the solution. The pain is the pitch."

PTL: Power, Trust, Likeness

The framework from course 2 for building a personal brand that creates opportunities, not just views.

"A brand is about teaching... it's teaching the associations you want your audience to have. Bad branding is when your audience makes those associations on your behalf. Good branding is when you make those associations for your audience. It's intentional."

If the goal is to influence (change behavior), people are primarily influenced by three things:

Power

Your ability to affect change in the world. Show you can do things.

  • Visually: recording in front of a mansion, a car, a fancy watch; or in front of your headquarters.
  • Verbally: "My portfolio has generated over $250M in aggregate revenue last year."
  • Power is relative — bartender example: inside the bar the bartender has power over you (controls the drinks); step outside and it evaporates. Same with an investor at a kid's soccer game: "you're just a parent."

Trust

"Trust is belief with evidence. Belief without evidence is called faith."

Built when: you tell someone to do something → they do it → a good result happens → they associate the value with you.

  • Example: Martha Stewart — for years she gave women step-by-step baking directions; women got compliments from friends/family and associated that value with Martha, so the next time she says "buy my cookbook/program," they do it.
  • Tactics: give step-by-step directions ("read these 50 marketing books," "use this tool to distribute your content," "use these hooks").
  • "We typically have high trust with our parents — for years we listened and got good results ('eat your veggies and you'll grow')."

Likeness

How similar you are to your audience — the in-group effect: we listen more to people in the same group we identify with; favoritism toward in-group, skepticism toward out-group.

Two dimensions:

  • External: how you talk, what you wear, how you look.
  • Internal: beliefs and values you signal.

"Whenever I'm doing marketing for a company, the first thing I do is get really clear on who are we targeting."

Application

"If you want to build an influential brand in 2026 and beyond, demonstrate power, trust and likeness over and over and over again in your content."

Reflection prompt: "What things do I need to be associated with, so that when my audience has a problem, they think of me first?"

Demand: Current vs Future

From course 2.

Two types of demand

"There are actually two different types of marketing. Future demand versus current demand."

  • Current demand — customers who want your product right now. Marketing that captures it = direct response / ads.
  • Future demand — customers who will want your product in the future. Created by having a brand (teaching associations — see PTL).

Car analogy: likelihood of buying a car in the next 2 weeks is low; in the next 10 years it's higher. The car company's goal isn't to hook you immediately — it's to build brand affinity so you think of them first when the buying window opens.

"Not everything that can be immediately measured matters. And not everything that can be measured matters." (the second clause is as he says it — measurable ≠ important; future demand work is systematically under-measured.)

Most marketers chase only current demand because it's easy to track; great marketers "dance" between the two.

Why a brand matters (business math)

A business survives on the LTV : CAC ratio (lifetime value per customer vs. cost to acquire a customer).

Building a brand improves both sides:

  • CAC side: one piece of content can reach millions → more leads; and those leads are pre-sold (they feel they know and trust you) → higher conversion.
  • LTV side: customers of a known brand pay more, buy more frequently, stay longer, give you the benefit of the doubt.

"201 version": a brand also improves LTGPE : CAC (lifetime gross profit per employee vs. cost to acquire talent) — you attract higher-quality talent who stay longer and are more aligned with your values.

The explore → exploit dance (courses 2 & 3)

"It's actually: explore with organic content, explore the angles that are resonating, and then exploit with paid ads."

Two circles: organic widens the outer circle (people aware of you); ads shrink into the inner circle (people ready to buy).

"This arbitrage opportunity... I don't know if this will be here forever. You probably have a three-year window to go super hard on organic content and then super hard on paid ads, because arbitrage opportunities go away."

Content Multiplication System

From course 2 (~00:10:52). Jay's "secret sauce" behind the prolific output of the top personal brands he works with.

Why start on X (Twitter)

  1. You're not penalized for posting volume (10+ posts/day).
  2. When a tweet does well, you know the idea (not the visual) is validated — so you can multiply it.

The three R's

Run on any outperforming post:

  1. Repost — republish it ~90 days later. "The majority of your audience hadn't seen the first post"; the people who did see both just need the core ideas repeated: "we need to be reminded about the core few things more than we need to be taught new things."
  2. Repurpose — take the validated idea/angle and reformat it for each platform as its own channel (Instagram, LinkedIn, ad hook, YouTube video, newsletter). Not a copy-paste: "treat every platform like a separate channel."
  3. Remix — rewrite the same post in many ways:
    • Same idea, different format: "Winners get better, losers get bitter" → "The fastest way to never get what you want is to be bitter when other people are winning."
    • Different idea, same format/template: template "X people do X, Y people do Y" → "Rich people focus on saving time, poor people focus on saving money."
    • "You can turn one idea into 10."

Hall of Fame

A living database of your best ideas:

  • Columns: name of the post, link to the post, when it was posted, whether it's already been reposted.
  • Include any post that beats your average engagement/impressions.

"By year five or six, you're basically just replaying your best hits... like when a musician goes on tour, they're not playing new songs, they're playing their already-proven songs."

Recap (course 2, ~00:14:29)

"Step one, build a hall of fame. Step two, use the three R's. If you master how to generate future demand, the world is yours."

The 5-Step Ad Writing Process

From course 3. The process Jay runs for every new client at Revenue Media Lab.

"The five steps I go through whenever I'm signing a new client to help them build their ads system."

Step 1 — Story Bible (~00:04:32)

A "universal source of truth": a living document containing who the client is (full life story), who the customers are, the offer, the funnel, the credibility bank (impressive results, frameworks), the avatar's top objections + where they hang out, required links, approved/banned words, allowed claims/guarantees, and voice examples.

  • Sources to build it: interview the client (or yourself), YouTube videos, podcasts — "in a 60-second reel you can hide behind a script, but a YouTube video is no-hiding."
  • AI assist: for a video-only creator, use Claude to download/transcribe their Instagram videos and build a voice skill.
  • Keep updating the Bible in real time as ads iterate.

Step 2 — "Why They Buy" sheet (~00:06:43)

"One of those tactics that are super juicy... download the client's sales calls, transcribe them, and pull out the key phrasings so we can say them back in the ads."

Mine for: specific phrasings of why they bought, dream outcomes, current pains/frustrations, and objections. Then build a PCP (Perfect Client Profile) — the customers who pay the most, stay longest, and who you most enjoy helping.

"This is the 20% that gets 80% of the returns. Identify the messaging that got these people in the door, and align that across the funnel."

No sales calls? Alternatives: competitor comment sections, Reddit threads, Amazon 3-star reviews ("typically the most truthful").

Step 3 — Research (~00:09:26)

"This is the magic sauce. This is the difference between okay copywriters and great copywriters."

Research checklist:

  • Market: is it validated? Three filters: (1) are people already paying (check Meta Ads Library for competitor ads running for months); (2) is there an untapped subgroup; (3) do they already know they have a problem. "A starving crowd" beats any other advantage (burger-stand thought experiment).
  • Offer: copy can only communicate the value the offer already has. Tweak via the three vectors of value — speed, risk, ease (fitness example: $10K/6-month gym program vs. one pill a day).
  • Awareness levels: five levels determine ad language.
  • Market sophistication: five stages.
  • Unique mechanism: see unique mechanism.
  • Big idea: see big idea.
  • Beliefs: customers must believe in the vehicle (does it work), internal (believe in themselves), external (will it work in my real life). Rotate these beliefs across ads so objections are pre-handled by checkout.

"Copy cannot create desire, it can only channel existing desire" (Eugene Schwartz). The best copywriters are "masters of psychology, not poets." Copywriting = clarity: who are we reaching, what do they want, how is our product the bridge.

Step 4 — Write the ad (~00:23:31)

Per ad: 5 hooks, 1 body, ~3 CTAs — "kaleidoscoping": test many hook angles on one body before opening a new ad set.

  • Hooks: where to find them (5 sources) + the 5-point checklist. Most writing time goes to hooks: Ogilvy — "five times as many people read the headline than the body... by the time you've written your headline you've spent 80 cents of your advertising dollar."
  • Hook sources: (1) your previous winning ads, (2) competitors' ads (Meta Ads Library), (3) your own organic content, (4) others' free content, (5) outlier tweets from "money Twitter."
  • Specificity sells (Claude Hopkins): "Platitudes and generalities roll off the human understanding like water off a duck's back."
  • Bodies: be specific in hooks, broader in bodies (reusable across hooks). Explanation formats that stick: stories, analogies, steps, memorable maxims ("If hard work was the answer, the donkey would own the farm"), whiteboard diagrams, podcast-style framing (Meta rewards ads that feel organic), qualifiers ("If you're a girl who owns a laptop...").
  • CTAs: "Who does what by when, and how." Example: "If you're a woman who wants to break into high ticket sales, then register for my free Zoom masterclass this Tuesday at 8pm ET... click below and save your spot."

Hook checklist (pre-ship)

  1. Is it a call-out or an open loop?
  2. Would our client say this out loud to a friend in a coffee shop?
  3. Would a stranger with zero context understand it?
  4. Is it free of insider language and buzzwords?
  5. Is it concrete and visualizable?

Step 5 — Ship & iterate

"Once we have all those assets in place, the ads actually kind of write themselves."

Double down on the winning branch; iterate on the winning angle rather than rebuilding new ads.

Key principles woven throughout course 3

  • Respect the reader: Ogilvy — "Your consumer is not a moron. It is your wife as best as possible." No fake urgency/hype; under-delivery spawns negative word-of-mouth "forever on the internet."
  • Damaging admission: "I'm 20 years old, I can't dance, I can't golf, I can't even drink yet... but the one thing I know with 100% confidence is that I am a dangerous copywriter."
  • Copy is a mall window: the goal is to see the items in the store, not admire the glass. "We are not trying to win poetry competitions... clear, concise, simple."
  • AI use: don't let AI write the copy (writing = thinking, alpha is in not outsourcing the writing); DO use AI for research, competitor analysis, and batch review ("you've written the same CTA for every ad — change some").
  • The pain is the pitch (shared with course 1 and course 2).

Awareness Levels (Schwartz)

From course 3 (~00:14:00). Five levels of consumer awareness that determine the language of your ads.

"There are five levels of awareness: unaware, problem-aware, solution-aware, product-aware, and most-aware."

LevelCustomer stateAd language
UnawareDoesn't know they have a problemEducate: name the problem. Example: "If you're over 45 and your back's been hurting for weeks — you may have a misplaced disk."
Problem-awareKnows the problem, not the solutionRecommend a specific solution and explain why: "You have tweaked-disk syndrome; we recommend this, not ice packs or couch rest."
Solution-awareKnows the problem and the range of solutionsDifferentiate: why your product is different/better (e.g. "show up once a week instead of three," "personal coach you can text," "they pick you up").
Product-awareKnows your specific productPresent the compelling offer.
Most-awareKnows everything, ready to buyJust show up with the offer; "skim off the top of the goodwill created by organic content."

"This is not something we actively use when we write ads each time" — but markets shift over time, so check where your market sits awareness-wise before writing.

Pair with market sophistication, which is about competitors, not the customer's knowledge.

Market Sophistication Stages

From course 3 (~00:16:54). Awareness levels describe the customer; sophistication describes the market — how many competitors there are and how worn-out the claims have become.

"There are five main stages of sophistication."

StageStateAd strategyPushup example
1No one has made the claim yetJust make the claim and win"Do pushups, you'll lose weight."
2Competitors show upMake a bigger claim"Do pushups, lose 50 lbs by tomorrow."
3Claims are worn outIntroduce a mechanism"Use the corkscrew pushup to lose 50 lbs."
4Competitors copy your mechanismImprove the mechanism"Corkscrew method + proprietary doctor-trusted pill, 50 lbs in 2 weeks."
5Market saturated; claims don't landIdentify with the person your customer wants to become — lean into personal brand/story"I grew up a really fat kid... tried everything... it wasn't until the corkscrew pushup + pill that I lost weight."

Identifying your stage

"Go to the Meta Ads Library, find 20 of your competitors' ads, and write down the claim in each. If they're all making the same claim, you're in stage 3+ and need a mechanism."

Strategy note: first-mover advantage & the #2 play

  • Be a first mover if you can (no competitors → stage 1).
  • If you're #2, use it: the 1962 Avis campaign — "Avis is number two. Why should you go with us? We try harder."
  • "When you have a big behemoth, you can outcompete them by being faster, delivering better results, or caring more."

Unique Mechanism

From course 3 (~00:19:19). The core research output for saturated markets.

"A unique mechanism is really just an explanation for why other solutions fail and why yours is better."

The finding formula

"The quickest way to find yours is to finish this sentence: Most people believe X, it is wrong because of Y, and the answer is actually Z."

"A good mechanism does four things: (1) explains why other solutions fail, (2) creates a believable path, (3) feels different, (4) is easy to understand. It's the frame you give to customers."

Worked example: the "money model" frame

Targeting business owners whose business isn't growing:

"Most people think it's a leads problem — you just need more customers. If we can say 'it's actually not that you lack customers; it's that you don't know how to monetize them. You don't have a money model' — now we've created a frame. When the problem persists, they'll think 'I have a money-model problem, I need to buy this book/program.'"

This is the frame behind the Money Models book (the $106M-in-3-days launch Jay helped write ads for — see about).

Worked example: accountability (former client, 7-figure fitness business)

"Most people think they stopped going to the gym and stopped getting results because they lack discipline. Actually it's because they don't have an accountability buddy."

He built an entire business on that frame: partner coaches text each student every morning and evening to confirm gym attendance, exercises, and diet.

"The way he was able to win is because he established that frame and used the unique mechanism of an accountability buddy to market to his customers."

Examples of the formula in action (course 1, ~00:15:28)

  • "Everyone thinks you have to start a business to make money online. It's wrong because you can get paid without being an influencer or building a whole business — it's called high-ticket sales."
  • "Everyone thinks more activity equals more progress. It's wrong because you scale faster by identifying the limiting factor and solving it — the value acceleration method."

Relation to the Six P's

The mechanism is the Process P: "How does the offer work? A good process explains why other solutions fail, creates a believable path, feels different, is easy to understand" (Six P's).

The Big Idea

From course 3 (~00:20:58).

"If you look at all the top creators or marketers in the space, they all have a big idea they use in their marketing."

Ogilvy:

"If you don't have a big idea, your advertising will pass like a ship in the night."

A big idea must be four things

  1. New — no one has said it before.
  2. Easy — one skill, one purchase.
  3. Safe — low risk, no need to quit everything.
  4. Big — it's about changing the trajectory of your life / future generations.

Case studies

De Beers — "Diamonds Are Forever"

"The most famous big idea in advertising... it completely changed culture. The reason people are obsessed with big diamond rings to display affection is because of this ad. It was new (diamond rings weren't a big thing before), easy (one purchase demonstrates your love), safe (lasts forever), and big (about the love of your life)."

Shelby Sap — "the Porsche Pilates lifestyle" (one of Jay's clients, about)

"She put a name to a feeling a lot of the women she markets to feel but couldn't articulate. New (no one talked about it), easy (learn one skill — high-ticket sales), safe (make sales on the side of your job, don't quit anything), big (change the trajectory of your life and future generations)."

How to build yours

  • Give a feeling a name and make it desirable.

Great Sentence Craft

From X article "I've Written 60,000 Tweets: How to Write a Great Sentence". Jay's 60,000-tweet practice, distilled.

What a great sentence is

A great sentence is the backbone of society: politicians, leaders, parents, and people all use sentences. "Everything you want out of life is on the other side of arranging the right words in the right sequence."

The 2x2 matrix of statements:

  • True + obvious — "You'll never create a masterpiece by accident."
  • True + non-obvious — the target. "If you make accidental decisions based on arbitrary advice from average people, you will end up with average outcomes at best."
  • False + obvious — "Everyone can create masterpieces as long as they believe it."
  • False + non-obvious — "Take advice from average people to get non-average results."

We want to say true things in non-obvious ways.

Jay checks every tweet with 2 questions: Is this true? Is this non-obvious? If it reads "yeah, I've heard that before," go one layer deeper. And if the reader would think "that's not true," it fails no matter how punchy.

The 6 bad-sentence failure modes (with rewrites)

  1. The motivational poster — trite, preachy. Rewrite: "It takes everyone a different amount of time to realize that harder problems are a good thing... more weight in the gym leads to muscle growth, more responsibility leads to mental growth."
  2. The buzzword pile — "99%," "mentor," "successes and failures." Rewrite: "The fastest way to accelerate your career is to get in rooms where your life goals are their minimum standards."
  3. The fortune cookie — vague, amorphous. Rewrite: "I will never bet against the person who continues to show up every day even when the rewards are not guaranteed."
  4. The textbook — too many commas, too many undefined terms. Rewrite: "People underestimate the power of creating space for stillness and wandering."
  5. The undefined abstraction — buzzwords without definitions. Rewrite: "The fastest way to spot a successful person: watch how quickly they make decisions, respond to messages, and turn ideas into action. Speed is king." (326K views on a 9-figure CEO's account)
  6. Contrast for the sake of it — em dashes read as AI, "you don't blah, you blah" with nothing underneath. Rewrite: "Your inability to start without certainty will hurt your potential far more than your lack of talent."

Fix: when a sentence is generic, chunk up (the bigger principle) or chunk down (details the reader can picture). Jay chunks down more often. For every vague word: "What the hell do I mean?"

The 8 writing rules

  1. Below a 3rd grade reading level.
  2. Present voice.
  3. Active, not passive.
  4. Strong verbs over adverbs.
  5. Usually one comma.
  6. Positive language.
  7. Cut redundant words.
  8. Cut unnecessary words (very, super, way, actually).

Screenshot those rules and use them as a checklist before you post.

The 9 rhetorical devices

Juxtaposition, contrast, before/after, repetition, alliteration, parallelism, metaphor/simile, antithesis, anadiplosis.

The rule: every device has to mean something. "You don't blah, you blah" with nothing underneath is one of the most common AI tells. If a device costs you clarity, go back to the plain sentence.

The 4 advanced moves

  1. Antithetical parallelism — repeat a structure, contrast two opposing ideas. "Lack of focus has killed more dreams than lack of talent." (252K views)
  2. The rule of three — 3 lines build tension, a 4th line breaks the pattern and delivers the payoff: "You can't get rich if you never risk losing money... The price of the life you want is the risks you're avoiding."
  3. Aspirational identity framing — name the identity people want, list the signaling behaviors.
  4. Specificity x utility — clear instructions get saved and shared.

More → Better → New

Twitter is just saying 1 thing 1,000 different ways.

The highest risk-adjusted return: do more of what works, make it better, only then try something new. Real numbers: "stick with the plan" idea did 361K views, the reworded version 288K; the speed tweet did 326K, the repost another 180K.

Most of your audience never saw the first version anyway. And the ones who did usually need reminders more than new lessons.

Exercises to do today

  1. Swipe file — collect great sentences and write down why they worked (2x2 box + device).
  2. Run the 2x2 on your last 10 posts: push "true but obvious" one layer deeper, cut the false.
  3. Name the failure on 5 weak sentences, then chunk down and rewrite.
  4. One idea, 5 ways — 5 core ideas × 5 devices = 25 sentences.
  5. Practice on X — post, measure, keep winners in a Hall of Fame and repost in 90 days.

  • The 6 Ps ([six-ps-of-copy.md]) are the thinking layer behind every sentence; the 5 Cs (clear, concise, concrete, conversational, cadence) are the execution layer.
  • A great sentence gives the reader something to do — that's the first step of the trust loop.

Head of Copy

From X article "Everything Is Copywriting: Why You Need to Hire a Head of Copy". Jay is the head of copy for a portfolio of 8-figure companies.

The core claim: everything is copywriting

If your media buyer keeps tweaking the targeting of your ads, but you're still unprofitable, you don't have a targeting issue. You have a messaging issue.

  • Make a list of every place the business uses words to get someone to do something: short-form scripts, carousels, stories, tweets, LinkedIn posts, newsletters, sequences, landing pages, webinars, VSLs, and every paid ad.
  • "A short-form video is a script before it's a video. A webinar is a sales letter you say out loud, and a landing page is a sales letter with buttons."
  • "Code is the backend of the internet. Copywriting is the front end."
  • In 2026, with AI making words cheap, the remaining alpha is distribution — and distribution runs on words.

Why copy matters more in 2026

  1. The copy is the targeting. Meta's Andromeda reads the ad's text and visuals, then targets people likely to respond. ~70–80% of spend runs as broad targeting; the creative and landing page do the targeting.
  2. Meta wants volume. For every $10k of spend, run 15–30 creative variations — but volume without quality is a waste: find a winning angle and iterate on it.
  3. AI made words cheap. The easier AI writing gets, the more alpha is in the thinking AI can't do. "AI can't sit on your sales calls."

Copywriting is thinking, not writing

Bad writing is almost always the result of muddy thinking. The game is won before the pen is used.

  • The best copywriters are researchers with a deep understanding of psychology, not poets with English degrees.
  • The 3-step business view: pick the right product/offer → find the right market → make it easy to want it. Most people think copy is only step 3.

What bad copy costs

  • The founder's own ads work at first; when handed to a junior writer or agency, quality drops every batch, and "generic ads find generic buyers."
  • Founders misdiagnose it as a media/targeting/creative problem: fire the buyer, change the video background, try a new agency — nothing changes.
  • "head of marketing" hires can turn out to be "a media buyer with a bigger title... not good at really anything other than media buying."
  • Pro tip: before blaming copy, check the market and the offer: "Great copy to people who don't want it loses to average copy to people who do. Copy is the last lever."

What good copy looks like

Before writing for a client, answer the 6 Ps of copy thinking (see six-ps-of-copy.md): people, positioning, promise, proof, priority, process.

  • The best ads came from transcribing customers' sales calls and using their exact words: "If you can describe your customers' pain better than they can, they'll assume you have the solution. The pain is the pitch."
  • Then write with the 5 Cs: clear, concise, concrete, conversational, cadence.

Micro examples (bad → good):

  1. Write the moment, not the thesis: "Are you broke?" → "Do you turn the brightness down on your phone when you open your bank app because you don't want other people to see your balance?"
  2. Call out who it's for: "Business owners, check this out." → "If you're a business owner doing over $1 million a year..." — the callout tells Meta (and the reader) who the ad is for.
  3. Concrete: "We help business owners grow." → "We took a roofing company from 14 leads a month to 70 leads a month."
  4. Specific promise: "Makes your home sparkle." → "Erases stains in under 30 seconds."
  5. Talk like a person: "Please don't hesitate to reach out." → "Just text me if you need anything."
  6. Fix the offer, then write it: "Give us $10,000 and we'll book you 5 qualified meetings every month for 90 days. Or we work for free until we do."

Why the founder can't keep writing it

"I'm either locked in on content or locked in on the business. Whichever one I'm locked in goes further."

  • The founder is the bottleneck: "I spend probably 20 of those hours on content... I'm the bottleneck."
  • Too close to it: "You can't read the label from inside the jar."
  • A hired writer still bounces every story, number, and opinion back to the founder: "by the time I'm reviewing it, I'm rewriting everything anyway."
  • Agencies pass work to juniors over time and write for one channel, so nothing says the same thing.

What a head of copy does (4 jobs)

  1. Positioning — the one-liner: "For [person], we're the [category] that [unique benefit], unlike [alternative]." If a stranger can't repeat it after hearing it once, it's too complicated.
  2. Messaging and strategy — builds the source of truth: a Story Bible (story, proof, beliefs), a Why They Buy sheet (customers' exact words), and a voice guide. Example: a trading-education client's buyers kept saying they "didn't want to guess, they wanted to know" → North Star angle: "Stop guessing."
  3. Day-to-day execution — writes or edits ads, scripts, emails, posts, pages, webinars, VSLs so every channel says the same thing in the format that fits it.
  4. The iteration loop — "If your best copywriter quit tomorrow, would your ads quit with them?" With a head of copy, the answer is no. Explore with organic, exploit with paid: validate ideas on plain text posts first, test ad angles with static images (so you know it's the angle, not the visuals), then double down on winners with video.

Signs you need one

  1. The founder still writes or approves every ad, email, and script.
  2. Ads plateaued; no time to dig into the messaging.
  3. Media buyer keeps changing targeting and the numbers don't move.
  4. Organic content, ads, emails, and sales calls all say slightly different things.
  5. You read your own content and cringe.
  6. Every new writer takes 3 months to "get" your voice, then leaves.

If you checked 2 or more, copy is probably one of the cheapest growth levers left in your business.

Exercises to do today (the 5 we run in the first weeks with every client)

  1. The copy audit — list every word-using touchpoint, who wrote it, when last updated. Watch for "nobody" and "a year ago."
  2. The Why They Buy sheet — transcribe 5 sales calls (or 10 reviews/DMs); keep exact words, never paraphrase; turn 5 quotes into 5 hooks.
  3. The competitor claim sweep — Meta Ad Library, 20 competitors; if 5 make the same claim, find the one none of them make.
  4. The point-at test — rewrite 5 claims with a number, timeframe, customer type, or specific outcome.
  5. Read it out loud — fix anywhere you stumble; if you wouldn't say it to a customer over coffee, rewrite it.

How the team does it (4 steps)

  1. Audit — find where copy is leaking money.
  2. Build the source of truth — Story Bible, Why They Buy sheet, voice guide; every word traces back to something true.
  3. Execute — from the founder's own words first, with an editor reviewing every draft before the founder sees it.
  4. Iterate — weekly; winners feed the next batch.

House rules: state the facts and tell the truth (undocumented numbers don't go in the copy); no fake urgency.

Trust Recession Content

From X article "The Trust Recession: How to Make Content That Builds Authority and Converts". For educational creators and business owners who use content to grow, not to entertain.

The asset changed

  • AI made content cheap: a LinkedIn post, thread, or YouTube script in under a minute. Lower barrier to entry → more supply, and most of it sounds the same.
  • "The asset used to be attention. But now attention is cheap." Anyone can buy attention with a controversial post, trend, or skit.
  • The true asset now is trust.

We are in a trust recession. There's more content than ever and less reason to believe any of it.

When trust is scarce, the people who have it win by a lot.

What trust is

Trust is belief with evidence. If you have belief without evidence, that's called faith.

Trust means people believe that listening to you will net them positive results. If earned before the sell, leads convert higher (pre-sold), customers pay more and stay longer, and they give you the benefit of the doubt.

The trust loop

Tell people to do stuff → they listen → they get good results → they tie the result to you. Next time you speak, they're more likely to comply.

Examples that run the loop:

  • Martha Stewart — decades of recipes: women baked, got compliments, and associated the good feeling with Martha, so they bought her cookbooks.
  • Parents — "drink milk and you'll grow tall" for years; the positive results built deep trust.
  • Content — someone reads an article, runs the PTL framework, gets clarity on brand positioning; the result is tied to Jay, so the next framework is more likely to be tried.

The question to ask: What step-by-step directions can I give my audience for them to follow and get positive results?

Procedural beats declarative

  • Declarative knowledge: what something is, why it matters. Procedural knowledge: how to do it, the steps.
  • Most internet content is declarative, so it skips the trust loop — there's nothing to do, so no result, no association.
  • Declarative: "Consistency is the key to growing on LinkedIn." You nod and keep scrolling.
  • Procedural: "Every Sunday, I block off 2 hours and write 2 weeks of content..." You can do that this Sunday — and if it works, who do you think of?
  • Pro tip: for educational content, use What / Why / How. What and why set it up; the how is what the reader can go do — that's where trust gets built.
  • Lower the perceived effort: the smaller the first step, the more people take it, the more people get results they tie back to you.

Why AI content makes you blend in

  • AI learned from the internet, and most of the internet is declarative, so it gives you the average: "Be authentic. Provide value. Stay consistent." Anyone could have written it.
  • "The messenger changes the message" — you bring your stories, mistakes, and results to an idea.
  • The Name Test: cover your name and photo; if someone couldn't tell it was you, it's too generic to post.

Earned secrets

An earned secret is something you only know because you paid for it — with time, reps, mistakes, money, or clients — so you can't Google it.

  • Writing about others builds an audience; writing about yourself (stories and learnings) builds authority.
  • Example: the 10,000-word "2027 Marketing Playbook" essay drove business because it took 6 years to learn those lessons — the 6 Ps, the content multiplication system, and the Why They Buy sheet all came from real work.

Collecting directions: pain points

Ask your audience — every pain point is a set of directions waiting to be written. 4 ways to collect:

  1. DM new followers.
  2. Run Q&A posts (be specific about the topic).
  3. Read your comments (a comment like "This won't work for me, I'm coming back from an injury" becomes the next post).
  4. Run free workshops; list every question from the transcript.

One pain point ("trying to land my first client") becomes 5 posts: 3 mistakes before you pitch; the exact way Jay landed his first client; the mistake that cost it; the hot take on portfolios; 5 signs of underpricing. 50 pain points = a few hundred pieces of procedural content.

Only talk about what you know

Don't make content on things you're not an expert in — the trust loop flips: bad result tied to you, less compliance next time. Two gates before posting:

  1. Do I know this super well, have I done it myself more than once?
  2. Do I have the proof (a result, mine or a client's)? If either is no: go get the reps and proof, or leave the topic to someone who has them. (Example: Publish30 — Jay had only ~7 months of LinkedIn writing, so he partnered with Kevin Shim for algorithm questions.)

The 2 banks → content

Every trust-building content piece needs 2 ingredients: a reason to listen (credibility) and something to go do (framework).

Exercise 1: The credibility bank

Write down every accomplishment, result, milestone, and number in one doc; pull from it when writing hooks. Jay's sample: weekly newsletter for 5 years, 60,000+ tweets, 500K+ audience, two businesses to six figures in 8 and 9 days, 2 best-selling books. Prompts when stuck: Results, Reps, People, Builds, Firsts and fasts. Rules: use the credibility that backs up the topic (newsletter stat for newsletter posts, not tweet count); lead with your own proof over name-dropping; keep adding to it.

Exercise 2: The frameworks bank

Frameworks are written-down mental models / SOPs / checklists AI can't copy, because the shortcut only comes after doing the work. Jay's: PTL, the 6 Ps, the 5 Cs, the 3 Rs + Hall of Fame, the story bank, the North Star filter. To create a framework in 5 steps: pick a repeated task → write down every step → cut to the 3–7 steps that move the result → give it a name (a name is a handle) → hand it to someone and verify the result.

Pairing and multiplication

Credibility + framework = valuable content.

In PTL terms: credibility shows power, the framework builds trust (they listen, they get a result), and the story of how you learned it builds likeness (they see themselves in where you started). 10 credibility points × 10 frameworks = 100 pairings; run each through the 3 R's (repost, remix, repurpose) and you have content for years. Formats: the how-to, the big brother/sister post, the public breakdown, the client story, the mistake post.

Turning trust into customers

  • Give your best stuff away; give, give, give, then ask — on X, promote about 1 in 15 posts.
  • One CTA: one CTA, one avatar, one offer. Say who it's for, what to do, when, and how.
  • It shows up as prospects booking calls that reference your posts, and founders asking how you're getting inbound.

Recap: the trust-building system

  1. Attention is cheap; the asset now is trust.
  2. Trust = belief + evidence.
  3. Trust builds in a loop: direction → listen → good result → association.
  4. Make procedural content; give people something to DO.
  5. Don't let AI write your insights — it gives the average, and the average blends in.
  6. Only talk about what you know super well and can prove.
  7. Build the credibility bank and the frameworks bank.
  8. Pair them: credibility + framework = valuable content.

I've Written 60,000 Tweets: How to Write a Great Sentence

A great sentence is the backbone of society as we know it. Politicians use sentences to get elected into office. Leaders use sentences to inspire their team. Parents use sentences to convince their kids to listen to them. People use sentences to show their love for each other. I believe everything you want out of life is on the other side of arranging the right words in the right sequence. The problem is that, especially with AI nowadays, everyone is outsourcing their thinking and writing to it, which is reducing their ability to craft great sentences. Automate only the skills you're willing to lose. But that's good news for you and me. If you commit to mastering the art of a great sentence, you'll be miles ahead of your peers. "Writing great sentences has changed my life:"

  • A cold email got me my first internship at beehiiv when I was 16.
  • "You Can Just Do Things" became a best-selling book.
  • Ads I helped write sold over $100 million in 3 days. Great sentences have landed me dream jobs. They've landed me dream clients. They've helped me build an audience. They've helped me write a book. The only thing they haven't done yet is find me a wife. I've written over 60,000 tweets. That's 60,000 reps at the same skill: squeezing one idea into 280 characters. And you can't compress an idea you don't have. By the end of this article, you'll know how to take an idea nobody cares about and turn it into a sentence nobody can ignore.

What is a great sentence?

Step one is to define what a great sentence is. I think of sentences in a 2x2 matrix:

  • True statements that are obvious
  • True statements that are non-obvious
  • False statements that are obvious
  • False statements that are non-obvious The same idea in each box:
  • True and obvious: "You'll never create a masterpiece by accident."
  • False and obvious: "Everyone can create masterpieces as long as they believe it."
  • False and non-obvious: "Take advice from average people to get non-average results."
  • True and non-obvious: "If you make accidental decisions based on arbitrary advice from average people, you will end up with average outcomes at best." We want to say true things in non-obvious ways. When I'm writing tweets, I often check them with 2 questions:
  1. Is this true?
  2. Is this non-obvious? A lot of tweets are true but obvious. And when something feels like "yeah, I've heard that before," it doesn't trigger much emotion or engagement. So go one layer deeper into the non-obvious truth. A few examples:
  • "You can beat most people by simply deciding what you want and then doing things on purpose to get it. That's it. Most people never even get that far."
  • "Few things feel emptier than finally getting something you never stopped to question if you even wanted in the first place."
  • "The most dangerous thing isn't a bad life, it's a good life you never chose. You'll fight to escape a bad life. A good one? You'll always find an excuse for never reaching for great." Pro tip: if someone in your niche would read your sentence and think "that's not true," it fails, no matter how punchy it is.

What makes a sentence bad?

One of the fastest ways to learn what a great sentence looks like is to study bad ones. Here are 6 tweets, what's wrong with each one, and how I'd rewrite them.

  1. The motivational poster
  • Trite. It sounds like a motivational poster in an elementary school classroom.
  • It's not even that motivational, just preachy.
  • Formatting note: you never want to split a sentence across lines like that, especially if you end on it. Hit enter twice instead. Rewrite: "It takes everyone a different amount of time to realize that harder problems are a good thing. It means you have more opportunity to grow. Just like more weight in the gym leads to muscle growth, more responsibility leads to mental growth."
  1. The buzzword pile
  • Too many buzzwords: "99%," "mentor," "successes and failures."
  • People tune out what sounds familiar. Rewrite: "The fastest way to accelerate your career is to get in rooms where your life goals are their minimum standards."
  1. The fortune cookie
  • I don't even know what this means.
  • Too vague and amorphous. It needs context.
  • You're not Naval. Rewrite: "I will never bet against the person who continues to show up every day even when the rewards are not guaranteed. Your potential is determined by the amount of uncertainty you're able to tolerate and how long you can tolerate it for."
  1. The textbook
  • Hard to read. Too many commas.
  • Too many words that need unpacking. What does "careful, logical reasoning" mean? What does "unbound exploration" mean? What is play?
  • Keep everything under a 3rd grade reading level. Rewrite: "People underestimate the power of creating space for stillness and wandering. Nearly all of my best ideas have come when I was away from my laptop doing something completely unrelated to what I was working on."
  1. The undefined abstraction
  • More buzzwords that need to be defined. What is urgency? How does it compound?
  • It lacks specificity. Rewrite: "The fastest way to spot a successful person: watch how quickly they make decisions, respond to messages, and turn ideas into action. Speed is king." That rewrite went live on a 9-figure CEO's account and did 326K views the first time we posted it.
  1. Contrast for the sake of it
  • The em dash reads as AI.
  • Buzzwords make people tune out again: grow, comfort, dirt.
  • It's the "you don't blah, you blah" template with nothing underneath.
  • I have no idea what I'm supposed to do with this information.
  • How do you get dragged through the dirt and stand up clean? That doesn't make sense. Don't use juxtaposition and contrast for the sake of it. The sentence still has to make sense. Rewrite: "Your inability to start without certainty will hurt your potential far more than your lack of talent. The reason entrepreneurs get paid the most is because they take on the most risk, uncertainty, and responsibility."

How do you fix a weak sentence?

When a sentence is generic, you have 2 options:

  • Chunk up: go broader, to the bigger principle.
  • Chunk down: add details the reader can picture. I chunk down more often. "Move fast" is vague. "Watch how quickly they make decisions, respond to messages, and turn ideas into action" gives the reader 3 actions they can picture someone doing. 3 fixes I've made, labeled by the problem: Too concise to land. Before: "You don't need accountability. You need integrity." After: "Most people don't need more accountability, they need more integrity. Accountability = doing the right thing because someone else is watching. Integrity = doing the right thing because you are watching." Motivational poster. Before: "Do one more when it hurts. That's the rep that counts." After: "In the gym, the last rep hurts the most and matters the most. In business, the hardest conversation hurts the most and pays the most." Too many undefined words. Before: "Small margins build massive gaps." After: "The older I get, the more I realize everything is details. Calling leads within 60 seconds vs. 60 minutes. One extra follow-up vs. stopping at three. Testing 10 headlines vs. settling for the first one." For every vague word, ask yourself, "What the hell do I mean?" Then run the sentence through my 8 writing rules:
  1. Below a 3rd grade reading level. ❌ We utilize leverage to gain higher returns on our equity checks. ✅ We borrow money so we can buy bigger stuff (and make more) than we could with our cash alone.
  2. Present voice. ❌ When you're creating your sales page ✅ When you create your sales page
  3. Active, not passive. ❌ The body was carried out of the room. ✅ We carried the body out of the room.
  4. Strong verbs over adverbs. Adverbs usually mean lame verbs. ❌ We shut the door really hard. ✅ We slammed the door.
  5. Usually one comma. If there are 2 commas, cut the middle or make 2 sentences. ❌ We tried to escape but the man, with the red mask, chased us until we lost our breath. ✅ The red-masked man chased us until we lost our breath. We were trapped.
  6. Positive language. ❌ Don't stop. ✅ Keep going.
  7. Cut redundant words. ❌ We simultaneously left the building at the same time. ✅ We left the building at the same time.
  8. Cut unnecessary words. Also cut very, super, way, and actually. ❌ He was able to get out of his car. ✅ He got out of his car. Screenshot those rules and use them as a checklist before you post.

What are rhetorical devices?

While I wish there was a silver bullet to craft a great sentence every single time, that's not how the world works. What I can give you is a toolbox. Rhetoric is the art of transferring information from one brain to another in the most effective way possible. Rhetorical devices are the techniques writers use to persuade, get an emotional reaction, or make a message more memorable. Here are the 9 I use the most:

  1. Juxtaposition: put 2 things side by side that don't usually go together. ❌ Reading old books is important for your creativity. ✅ If you want new ideas, read old books.
  2. Contrast: set what the reader thinks they need against what they need. ❌ When you have trouble sleeping, it's often due to your lack of purpose. ✅ You don't need more sleep, you need a better reason to get out of bed.
  3. Before/After: show where you were, then where you are now. ❌ Fear is a sign to lean in. ✅ I used to be terrified of putting myself out there. But every great thing in my life has been a result of embracing discomfort and doing it anyway. Fear is a sign to lean in.
  4. Repetition: repeat a word or phrase so the point hits harder each time. ❌ We must act quickly, with determination and unity. ✅ We must act now. We must act decisively. We must act together.
  5. Alliteration: start nearby words with the same sound. ❌ Fake friends lie to you. ✅ Fake friends make false promises.
  6. Parallelism: give every item in a list the same shape. ❌ She likes reading, to write, and skiing. ✅ She likes reading, writing, and skiing.
  7. Metaphor/Simile: explain an idea by comparing it to something the reader already knows. ❌ Overthinking is bad. You need to take real action. ✅ Trying to think your way out of overthinking is like trying to snort your way out of a crack addiction.
  8. Antithesis: put opposite ideas in one balanced sentence. ❌ Want to stand out? Get started. ✅ The quickest way to get ahead is to get started.
  9. Anadiplosis: end one phrase with a word, then start the next phrase with that same word. ❌ Fear is dangerous. Anger is destructive. Hate corrupts you. ✅ Fear leads to anger. Anger leads to hate. Hate leads to suffering. The rule: every device has to mean something. "You don't blah, you blah" with nothing underneath is one of the most common AI tells. If a device costs you clarity, go back to the plain sentence.

The 4 advanced moves

These are the 4 I lean on the most when I write for founders.

  1. Antithetical parallelism. Parallelism: you repeat a sentence structure ("Lack of X has killed more dreams than lack of Y"), which creates rhythm and clarity. Antithesis: you contrast 2 opposing ideas ("focus" vs. "talent") inside that structure to show which one matters. So instead of saying "Focus is more important than talent," say: "Lack of focus has killed more dreams than lack of talent." That one did 252K views on the CEO's account.
  2. The rule of three. Ideas in groups of 3 are more memorable and satisfying. Three creates rhythm and a sense of completeness: beginning, middle, end. Use 3 lines to build tension and expectation. Then use a fourth line to break the pattern and deliver the payoff. So instead of saying "You have to risk what you have to get what you want," you can say: "You can't get rich if you never risk losing money. You can't get loved if you never risk getting rejected. You can't get strong if you never risk getting injured. The price of the life you want is the risks you're avoiding."
  3. Aspirational identity framing. This is when you describe a behavior through the lens of a group people want to belong to:
  4. Name a desirable identity (successful person, high performer, disciplined, rich).
  5. List the behaviors that signal that identity (decide fast, respond fast, take action).
  6. Readers compare themselves to that standard.
  7. If they aspire to that identity, they lean in and engage. So instead of saying "Move fast and get things done quickly," you write: "The fastest way to spot a successful person: watch how quickly they make decisions, respond to messages, and turn ideas into action."
  8. Specificity x utility. Clear instructions feel useful, and useful content gets saved and shared.
  9. Start with a common situation people struggle with (fear, doubt, bad days).
  10. Give one specific action that improves that situation.
  11. The reader leaves feeling they can apply it today. For example: "Pro tip: If you feel restless before bed, write down the tasks you plan to do tomorrow. Listing them gives your brain a sense of closure and makes it easier to relax. I call it 'closing the loop.'"

How do you write more great sentences?

Once you find a true idea that resonates, say it again in different ways. Twitter is just saying 1 thing 1,000 different ways. There are 2 ways to remix a sentence:

  1. Same structure, different topic. "Rich people buy time. Poor people buy stuff" becomes "Winners get better. Losers get bitter."
  2. Same topic, different structure. "Rich people buy time. Poor people buy stuff" becomes "If you want to build wealth, buy time. If you want to stay poor, buy stuff." I think of it as More → Better → New. In business, the highest risk-adjusted return comes from doing more of what works, then making it better, and only then trying something new. The same applies to sentences. For the CEO I wrote for, it looked like this:
  • "Sooner or later you realize everything comes down to your ability to stick with the plan despite whatever emotions you may feel" did 361K views.
  • A later batch said it again: "You become an adult when you realize everything eventually comes down to your ability to stick with what you said you'd do despite whatever emotions you may feel." That one did 288K.
  • The speed tweet did 326K views the first time. When we reposted it, it did another 180K. Most of your audience never saw the first version anyway. And the ones who did usually need reminders more than new lessons.

Exercises to do today

Exercise 1: Collect great sentences. Start a swipe file. Every time a sentence stops your scroll, makes you laugh, or makes you buy, save it. Then write down why it worked: which box of the 2x2 it lives in and which device it uses. Exercise 2: Run the 2x2 on your last 10 posts. Put each one in a box. Every "true but obvious" sentence gets pushed one layer deeper. Every "false" sentence gets cut. Exercise 3: Name the failure. Take 5 weak sentences (yours or anyone's) and name what's wrong with each: motivational poster, buzzwords, too vague, too many commas, undefined words, or contrast for the sake of it. Then rewrite each one by chunking down. Exercise 4: One idea, 5 ways. Pick 5 core ideas you believe. Write each one 5 different ways, each with a different device from the toolbox. That's 25 sentences. Exercise 5: Practice on X. X gives you quick feedback loops. Post a sentence, see what happens, and keep the winners in a Hall of Fame so you can say them again in 90 days.

Everything Is Copywriting: Why You Need to Hire a Head of Copy

I'm the head of copy for a portfolio of 8-figure companies. I write hundreds of ads every month. And the most common complaint I hear from business owners is, "Our targeting is off." It almost never is. If your media buyer keeps tweaking the targeting of your ads, but you're still unprofitable, you don't have a targeting issue. You have a messaging issue. In 2026, the copy is the targeting. And it's not only your ads. Your short-form scripts, carousels, emails, landing page, webinar, and VSL are all copy too. Everything is copywriting. Which means every business already has a head of copy. For most of them, it's the founder at 11pm, or a junior writer who's guessing. In this essay, I want to break down what copywriting is, why it matters more in 2026 than it ever has, what good and bad copy look like, the exercises I'd do today, and why I think the next hire you make should be a head of copy.

What is copywriting?

When I first heard of copywriting, I thought it was the little C logos you see next to trademarks. It's not. Copywriting is the art of transferring information from brain A to brain B in the most effective way possible. It's persuasive writing. In business, that looks like 3 steps:

  1. Pick the right product, service or offer.
  2. Find the right market (the people who need it).
  3. Make it easy for those people to understand why they should want it. Most people think copywriting is only step 3: clever words and a good hook. But the best copywriters I know aren't poets with English degrees. They're researchers with a deep understanding of psychology. Copywriting is thinking, not writing. Bad writing is almost always the result of muddy thinking. If you skip the thinking, it's like grabbing a shovel and digging as fast as you can before you've checked whether you're in the right spot. You can dig a beautiful hole, but it doesn't matter if it's in the wrong place. The game is won before the pen is used.

Why is everything copywriting?

Make a list of every place your business uses words to get someone to do something. On the organic side:

  • Short-form scripts
  • Carousels
  • Instagram story sequences
  • Tweets
  • LinkedIn posts
  • Email newsletters
  • Email sequences
  • Landing pages
  • Webinars
  • VSLs On the paid side: every ad you run. A short-form video is a script before it's a video. A webinar is a sales letter you say out loud, and a landing page is a sales letter with buttons. Code is the backend of the internet. Copywriting is the front end. And in an age where AI can build almost anything, the remaining valuable alpha is the ability to create distribution, and distribution runs on words.

Why does copy matter more in 2026?

  1. The copy is the targeting. Before, you needed crazy targeting to figure out who you were reaching, where they lived, and what their demographics were. Now Meta has an update called Andromeda, which is basically Meta's AI system that does a lot of the targeting for you. It reads the text you write and the visuals and verbal stuff you include, shows the ad to the people it thinks will respond, and then, based on their impressions and engagement, sends it to more of those people or to other people. So if your ad says "I'm looking for healthcare business owners," Meta starts sending your ad to healthcare business owners. About 70 to 80% of our spend is broad targeting, with almost no interest targeting and barely any lookalikes. The creative and the landing page tell Meta who will respond.
  2. Meta wants volume. For every $10k you spend, you want 15 to 30 creative variations. But volume without quality is a waste of time. It's not about recording in a different background, changing the person's shirt, or flipping the camera. The copy is the targeting, and the copy is the messaging. Find a winning angle and iterate on that angle.
  3. AI made words cheap. Anyone can open a chat window and get an ad, an email and a landing page in under a minute. The easier it is to use AI to write, the more alpha there is in the thinking AI can't do. AI can't sit on your sales calls. It can't uncover the hidden facts about your business that make you different. It gives you the average of everything it's read, and the average blends in.

What does bad copy cost you?

At the start, the founder writes the ads. And they work, because they sound like the founder and they speak to the right buyer. Then the business grows, the founder runs out of time, and they hand it to a copywriter or an agency. The first batch is fine. But the writer doesn't know your story, customers, or how you talk, so every batch they guess a little more. A few months later, the ads sound like everyone else's. And since the copy is the targeting, generic ads find generic buyers. I hear this on sales calls every week. Here are a few lines founders have told me, word for word:

  • "The ads are not doing what we need because we don't have the right ad copy... so we're just kind of stuck."
  • "Before is like the founder used to write everything, but after he tried to like hire people, then the quality started to drop."
  • "People could tell, no, dude, you didn't write this. And to me, it's like, dude, our brand's burning."
  • "We only spent like 50k on ads for it... ironically did 20k from ads." Most of them don't see it as a copy problem at first. They see it as a media buying, targeting, or creative problem. So they fire the media buyer, change the background of the video, try a new agency, and nothing changes. One founder put it better than I could: "Every 30 days, if sales are stagnant, we'll try this thing, then that doesn't really make a difference. We'll try something else. And ultimately, I just keep coming back to the same thing, which is, it all comes down to copywriting." Another founder told me he'd hired a "head of marketing" who turned out to be a media buyer with a bigger title. "Like what does that really mean? That means like copywriter and media buyer... they actually are not good at really anything other than media buying." You don't have a media buying or targeting issue. You have a copy issue. Pro tip: before you blame the copy, check the market and the offer. The market outweighs any piece of copy you can write. Great copy to people who don't want it loses to average copy to people who do. Check 3 layers in order: who you sell to, what you sell, and how you sell it. Copy is the last lever.

What does good copy look like?

Before I write anything for a client, I answer 6 questions. I call them the 6 Ps of copy thinking:

  1. People: Who is it for?
  2. Positioning: Why you over every other option?
  3. Promise: Where are you taking them?
  4. Proof: Why should they believe you?
  5. Priority: Why now?
  6. Process: How does it work, and why is it different? The answers come from research. Some of the best ads I've ever written came from downloading our customers' sales calls and transcribing them, then using their exact words. Because if you can describe your customers' pain better than they can, they'll assume you have the solution. The pain is the pitch. Then I write with the 5 Cs: clear, concise, concrete, conversational, and cadence.

Good copy vs bad copy: micro examples

  1. Write the moment, not the thesis. Bad: "Are you broke?" Good: "Do you turn the brightness down on your phone when you open your bank app because you don't want other people to see your balance?" The second one is a moment your reader has lived, so they can picture it.
  2. Call out who it's for. Bad: "Business owners, check this out." Good: "If you're a business owner doing over $1 million a year..." Most writing yells "hey!" at everyone, which means it's addressed to no one. Since the copy is the targeting, the callout is how Meta (and the reader) knows who the ad is for.
  3. Make it concrete. Bad: "We help business owners grow." Good: "We took a roofing company from 14 leads a month to 70 leads a month." Clear gets understood. Concrete gets remembered.
  4. Make the promise specific. Bad: "Makes your home sparkle." Good: "Erases stains in under 30 seconds."
  5. Talk like a person. Bad: "Please don't hesitate to reach out." Good: "Just text me if you need anything."
  6. Fix the offer, then write it. Copy can only communicate the value your offer already has. We don't make things up. Here's an offer I rewrote: Before: "Give us $10,000 and we'll reach out to 5 people a month for you for a year." After: "Give us $10,000 and we'll book you 5 qualified meetings every month for 90 days. Or we work for free until we do." Basically the same service. But a far more valuable offer. None of the good versions are fancier, only more specific. And you only get specific by doing the research and the thinking first.

Why can't the founder keep writing it?

Because you can't do both. One founder told me on a call, "I'm either locked in on content or locked in on the business. Whichever one I'm locked in goes further." Another one said he only works about 25 hours a week, "but I spend probably 20 of those hours on content... I'm the bottleneck." And even when you have the time, you're too close to it. As another founder told me, "You can't read the label from inside the jar." Once you're doing over $100k a month, every hour you spend writing hooks is an hour you're not spending on the business. So you hire a writer and hand them a doc of ideas. But you're still the bottleneck, because the writer has to come back to you for every story, number, and opinion. Or, as one founder told me, "by the time I'm reviewing it, I'm rewriting everything anyway." Why not just hire an agency? A lot of agencies feel easy at the start, because they take care of everything. The downside is that eventually they pass you off to junior people, and quality goes down. Now you're stuck with them. And they write for one channel. The ads agency writes your ads, someone else writes your emails and scripts, and nobody makes sure it all says the same thing. One founder I talked to described exactly the role he was missing (someone to own his reels, stories, emails, webinars, and funnels) and then named it himself: "a pretty big role, like head of copy, if you will."

What does a head of copy do?

A head of copy owns 4 jobs: positioning, messaging and strategy, the day-to-day execution, and the iteration loop.

  1. Positioning. How your business is perceived in the customer's mind, and why you over every other option. This is the one-liner: "For [person], we're the [category] that [unique benefit], unlike [alternative]." If a stranger can't repeat it back after hearing it once, it's too complicated. If you confuse, you lose.
  2. Messaging and strategy. What you say, to who, and in what order. This is where the research lives. The head of copy builds the source of truth every writer works from: a Story Bible (your story, proof, and beliefs), a Why They Buy sheet (your customers' exact words for their pain, dreams, and why they bought), and a voice guide (how you sound, and the words you'd never use). In practice, we look at who your best customers are (the ones who pay the most, stay the longest, and we enjoy serving the most). We take their exact phrasings from when they joined. Then we make sure that messaging shows up across the rest of the funnel on purpose. For one client in trading education, the pattern between the people who bought was a phrase they kept saying: they didn't want to guess, they wanted to know. That became the North Star angle for everything: "Stop guessing."
  3. Day-to-day execution. The head of copy writes or edits the ads, scripts, emails, posts, landing pages, webinars, and VSLs, so every channel says the same thing in the format that fits it.
  4. The iteration loop. Copy is never done. The head of copy looks at what's working, figures out why, and feeds it back in. Explore with organic, and exploit with paid. If a tweet or LinkedIn post does well, it's probably because the idea behind it did well, since there are no fancy graphics or visuals. So we validate the idea, then ask how we can say that one core idea in a thousand different ways. On the ad side, we test a lot of angles using static images first, because then you know it's the angle and not the visuals. Then we double down on the winners with video. Which brings me to the question I'd ask every business owner reading this: If your best copywriter quit tomorrow, would your ads quit with them? With a head of copy, the answer is no. If a writer leaves, the next one starts with everything the last one learned.

How do you know if you need one?

Here are the signs I look for:

  1. You (the founder) still write or approve every ad, email, and script.
  2. Your ads plateaued, or you know they could be more profitable but you don't have time to dig into the messaging.
  3. Your media buyer keeps changing the targeting, and the numbers don't move.
  4. Your organic content, ads, emails, and sales calls all say slightly different messages.
  5. You read your own content and cringe.
  6. Every new writer takes 3 months to "get" your voice, then leaves. If you checked 2 or more, copy is probably one of the cheapest growth levers left in your business.

Exercises to do today

Even if you never hire anyone, do these 5. They're the same exercises we do in the first weeks with every client. Exercise 1: The copy audit. Open a Google Doc and list every place your business uses words to get someone to do something. Every ad, email, script, page, post, and slide. Next to each one, write who wrote it and when it was last updated. Watch for 2 patterns: how long the list is, and how many lines say "nobody" or "a year ago." Exercise 2: Build a Why They Buy sheet. Download 5 sales calls (or 10 customer reviews or DMs). Transcribe them. Then copy out every line where a customer describes their problem, what they tried before, what they want, and why they bought. Keep their exact words, including typos, filler, and half-finished sentences. Never paraphrase. Then turn 5 of those quotes into 5 hooks. For example, a customer saying "With my kids in school, I feel like I finally have the time to do something for myself" became "If you've been looking for a way to make money around your kids' schedule without starting a whole business, you need to hear this." Exercise 3: The competitor claim sweep. Open the Meta Ad Library and pull up 20 competitors. Write down the main claim behind each ad. If 5 of them are making the same claim, your market has heard it, so stop making that claim and look for the one none of them make. Exercise 4: Rewrite 5 claims so you can point at them. Imagine your reader can't hear you. You can't point at "proven results." You can point at a number. Take 5 claims from your website or ads and rewrite each one with a number, a timeframe, a name of a type of customer, or a specific outcome. If you can't, you know what proof to go get. Exercise 5: Read it out loud. Read your best ad and your landing page out loud. Fix anywhere you stumble. Then ask: would I say this to a customer over coffee? If not, rewrite it until you would.

How we do this for clients

The Trust Recession: How to Make Content That Builds Authority and Converts

~4,000 words. Save this for later if you don't have 15 minutes :)

Why is it harder to grow a business with content now, when it's never been easier to make it?

AI has made it easier to make more content. You can open a chat window and get a LinkedIn post, a tweet thread and a YouTube script in under a minute. The barrier to entry has never been lower. And when the barrier to entry drops, supply goes up. There's more content going out today than at any point in history, and most of it sounds the same. The asset used to be attention. But now attention is cheap. Anyone can get attention by posting something controversial, hopping on a trend, or filming a skit. Attention isn't the hard part anymore. The true asset now is trust. This essay is for educational creators and business owners who want to use content to grow their business, not to entertain people. If your content gets views but doesn't get you clients, customers or calls, this is probably why. In this essay, I want to explain what trust is, how it gets built, why most content (and almost all AI content) can't build it, and the 2 exercises I'd do today so you can make content only you can make.

What is trust?

Trust is belief with evidence. If you have belief without evidence, that's called faith. When I say someone trusts you, I mean they believe that listening to you will net them positive results. If you can earn people's trust before you sell to them, it'll make everything easier:

  • Leads convert at a higher rate, because they feel like they know, like and trust you. They're already pre-sold.
  • Customers are willing to pay more, buy more often and stay longer.
  • They give you the benefit of the doubt. When anyone can generate content, the people consuming it get more skeptical. They've seen the same "7 lessons from Elon Musk" thread written 100 different ways. We are in a trust recession. There's more content than ever and less reason to believe any of it. That's bad news if you make generic content. It's great news if you don't, because when trust is scarce, the people who have it win by a lot.

So how do you get trust?

You have to tell people to do stuff, they have to listen, and they have to get good results. That's it. If I give you a direction, say "drink more water," and you do it, and you get a positive result (you're not thirsty and you feel more energized), you associate that good result with me. So the next time I tell you to do something, the likelihood you comply goes up. Then you do the next one, it works again, and the association gets stronger. Why does Martha Stewart have so much trust? For pretty much decades, Martha Stewart gave the women in her audience step-by-step directions to get a positive result. She gave them recipes. These women would follow them, and they'd bake something good. Then they'd get compliments from their spouses, neighbors, and kids. They'd associate that good feeling with Martha. So the next time she told them to do something (buy my cookbook, buy my course, whatever), these women listened. Why do you trust your parents? This is also why our parents have a ton of trust with us. For years they told us: drink milk and you'll grow tall. Eat your veggies and you'll be healthy. Don't touch the fire. And for years we listened and got positive results. So we have high trust with our parents. What does this look like with content? Someone read one of my articles, did the PTL framework and got clarity on their brand positioning. I gave a direction (here's a framework, run your brand through it), they listened and got a result, and now that result is tied to me. So the next time I post a framework, they're more likely to try it. So the question to ask yourself is: What step-by-step directions can I give my audience for them to follow and get positive results? Because you want that association in their head. "Hey, listening to this person gives me positive results."

Why doesn't most content build trust?

There are 2 types of knowledge:

  • Declarative knowledge is knowledge ABOUT something. What it is, why it matters.
  • Procedural knowledge is knowledge on HOW to do something. The steps. Most content on the internet is declarative, and not enough is procedural. Let's go back to the trust loop. For trust to get built, I have to tell you to do something, you have to do it, and you have to get a result. Declarative content skips the first step, because there's nothing to do, so there's no result and no association with you. For example: Declarative: "Consistency is the key to growing on LinkedIn." You read it, nod and keep scrolling, and nothing changes. Procedural: "Every Sunday, I block off 2 hours and write 2 weeks of content. I open a Google Doc and write down 6 categories: far past, recent past, current, future, client stories and hypotheticals. For the far past, I scroll back through my photo album (1 year ago today, 2 years ago, 5 years ago). For the recent past, I open my Google Calendar and look at last week's meetings and tasks. Then I write one post for each idea." You can do that this Sunday. And if you do it and you end up with 2 weeks of content, who do you think of? Procedural content makes people DO something, and doing something is how they get a result they can tie back to you. Pro tip: you don't have to cut declarative content. For educational content, I use What / Why / How. The what and the why set it up. The how is the part people can go do, and that's where the trust gets built.

How do you make your content more procedural?

  1. Write the body as What / Why / How.
  • What: the point you're trying to make.
  • Why: why people should care.
  • How: how they implement it. For example: you should drink more water (what). It helps you think more clearly, speak more articulately, makes your skin glow and gives you more energy (why). Before every meal, drink an 8 oz glass of water, which adds up to 24 ounces a day and is more than enough to get started (how).
  1. Make it concrete. Make your writing visualizable, so your reader can picture it. Instead of "we help business owners grow," say "we took a roofing company from 14 leads a month to 70 leads a month." Instead of "post consistently," say "every Sunday, block off 2 hours."
  2. Lower the perceived effort. Perceived effort is how much effort it takes to read your piece AND to implement it. The smaller the first step, the more people take it, and the more people take it, the more people get a result they tie back to you.

Why does AI content make you blend in?

If you use AI to make your content, it will put out shitty, surface-level declarative knowledge. I hate using AI to write, but I love using AI for everything else. The problem is what AI learned from. It learned from the internet, and most of the internet is declarative. So when you ask it for a post on "how to build a personal brand," it gives you the average of every post on building a personal brand. Be authentic. Provide value. Stay consistent. None of that is wrong, but anyone could have written it. This will make you blend in. And when you blend in, you have a weak brand, because nothing you post gives anyone a reason to remember you over the other 1,000 accounts saying the same stuff. If you're a robot spitting out educational information, that's great, but you also sound like AI. People don't only want to learn information. They want to connect with the messenger. This is why the market for content can't be saturated. The messenger changes the message. Listening to an idea from me is different than listening to the same idea from someone else, because I bring my stories, mistakes and results to it. Here's a test I run on everything I write. I call it the Name Test: cover your name and your photo. Could someone tell it was you? If not, it's too generic to post.

So what should you make content about?

Instead of AI's average, you want to use your own deep insights, your own earned secrets and the specific knowledge you picked up from years of doing it and helping other people do it. An earned secret is something you only know because you paid for it. You paid with time, reps, mistakes, money or clients, so you can't Google it. I'll give you an example. I put out a 10,000-word essay the other week, The 2027 Marketing Playbook. It drove a lot of business. People said it was the best article they'd read and that it wasn't AI slop. Wanna know why they liked it? Because it took me 6 years to learn those lessons. The frameworks in that essay came from work I did. The 6 Ps are the process I run before I write anything for a client. The content multiplication system is how I've posted 60,000+ tweets. The why they buy sheet came from transcribing client sales calls and pulling out the words customers used. When you write about others, you build an audience. When you write about yourself, your stories and learnings, you build authority. This is why I'm not a big fan of the "here's what Elon Musk did, and here are 7 lessons for you" posts, because they can get reach, but they don't prove you know anything.

How do you know which directions to give?

Ask your audience. They'll tell you exactly what they're stuck on, and every pain point is a set of directions waiting to be written. 4 ways to collect pain points:

  1. DM new followers. "Hey, saw you followed me. Just want to introduce myself. Why'd you follow me, and what are you interested in learning?" People will tell you.
  2. Run Q&A posts. "I'm free for the next hour, ask me anything about writing on LinkedIn." Be specific about the topic, or people won't know what to ask.
  3. Read your comments. If I post a marathon training plan and someone comments, "This won't work for me, I'm coming back from an injury," that's my next post.
  4. Run free workshops. Get 30 people on a Zoom, have them ask questions, then pull the transcript and list every question that came up. Put them all in one doc. Then turn each pain point into content. Say someone DMs me, "I'm trying to land my first client and I'm struggling." That one pain point turns into:
  • Here are 3 mistakes to fix before you pitch.
  • Here's the exact way I landed my first client.
  • Here's the mistake that cost me my first client, and what I'd do instead.
  • Hot take: you don't need a portfolio to land your first client.
  • 5 signs you're underpriced on your first client. If you collect 50 pain points, that's a few hundred pieces of procedural content, and every one of them is something your audience told you they need.

What happens when you talk about stuff you don't know?

Anyone can pick up a phone and hit record. Don't make content on things you're not an expert in. You'll lose credibility. Think about the trust loop again. If you give someone a direction outside your expertise, they follow it, and it doesn't work, the trust loop flips. Now they associate a bad result with you, and the next time you tell them to do something, they're less likely to listen. Only talk about topics you know super well and have the proof to back up your claims. This is how you win. I'll give you an example from my own business. When I started Publish30 (my 30-day LinkedIn writing cohort), I'd only been writing on LinkedIn for about 7 months. I'd learned a lot about the LinkedIn algorithm, but I was by no means an expert. So I partnered with Kevin Shim, who has over 65,000 followers on LinkedIn and ran a ghostwriting agency. Kevin is the person I lean on for the algorithm questions (What's the best time to post? Should I use this feature? Should I be using pictures?) Before you post something, ask yourself 2 questions:

  1. Do I know this super well? Have I done it myself, more than once?
  2. Do I have the proof? Can I point to a result, mine or a client's? If the answer to either one is no, either go get the reps and the proof, or leave the topic to someone who has them.

How do you find your earned secrets?

So... how do you figure out what you know that's worth sharing? 2 exercises for you to implement right now. Fundamentally, every piece of trust-building content needs 2 ingredients:

  1. A reason to listen to you (your credibility).
  2. Something to go do (your framework). The 2 exercises below build a bank of each.

Exercise 1: Build your credibility bank

Write down every single cool accomplishment you've done that you can use in your content. Every result, milestone, and number. Anything someone else would find impressive. Put it all in one doc. Then pull from it every time you write a hook. Here's part of mine:

  • I've written a newsletter every single week for the past 5 years.
  • I've written over 60,000 tweets.
  • I've grown my audience to 500,000+ people.
  • I scaled my new business to six figures in 8 days.
  • I scaled a second business to six figures in 9 days.
  • I've written 2 best-selling books. Why does this matter? Because credibility is the reason someone listens to you in the first place. In my hook formula, credibility sits on top next to benefit and relevance. It answers the reader's first question: why should I listen to you? If you're stuck, run through these prompts:
  1. Results: What numbers have I hit? Revenue, followers, clients, weight lifted, miles run.
  2. Reps: What have I done a lot of? Posts written, calls taken, ads tested, years in the game.
  3. People: Who have I helped, and what happened for them?
  4. Builds: What have I made? Products, businesses, books, courses, teams.
  5. Firsts and fasts: What did I do younger, faster or with less than people expect? Use the credibility that backs up the topic. If you're writing about newsletters, use the newsletter stat, not the tweet count. Dumping every accomplishment into one hook reads as insecure, and it buries the one number that matters. Lead with your own proof. For years, I leaned on the names of people I'd worked with. It worked up to a point. But at some stage, constant name-dropping gets annoying, and it weakens your brand because it's someone else's proof. These days I reach for my own results first and ask, "What can I, and only I, uniquely say?" Keep adding to it. Every time you get a win, a client result or a nice DM, add it to the bank. In a year, you'll be surprised how long it gets.

Exercise 2: Build your frameworks bank

Next, write down your frameworks bank. These are SOPs, steps, checklists and exercises that you use, or that you give to clients or your team, to get results. A framework is a mental model written down. And a mental model is a mental shortcut to do things easier because you've done it so much. AI can't copy that, because you only find the shortcut after you've done the work enough times to know which steps matter. Here's part of mine:

  • PTL (power, trust, likeness): the 3 ways people get influenced.
  • The 6 Ps of copy thinking (people, positioning, promise, proof, priority, process): what I answer before I write a word for a client.
  • The 5 Cs (clear, concise, concrete, conversational, cadence): how I write.
  • The 3 Rs (repost, remix, repurpose) + the Hall of Fame: how I post a ton on every platform.
  • The story bank: 5 minutes at the end of every day writing down what happened, so I always have stories to write about.
  • The North Star filter: the 2 questions I run every big decision through. What do I want, and what inputs increase the likelihood I get it? If you don't have any frameworks, then you need to create some. Here's how I'd start:
  1. Pick something you do over and over for your clients, your team or yourself. Onboarding a client. Writing a sales page. Running a workout.
  2. Write down every step the next time you do it. Don't clean it up yet.
  3. Cut it down to the steps that move the result. Usually it's 3 to 7.
  4. Give it a name. A name is a handle. People remember PTL, but hardly anyone's going to remember "the process I use for influence."
  5. Hand it to someone and see if they get the result. If they do, you have a framework. If they don't, find the step they got stuck on and fix it.

How do you turn the 2 banks into content?

Once you have those, you can pair credibility with a framework, and boom, now you have a piece of valuable content. Credibility + framework = valuable content. Your credibility gives people a reason to listen, and your framework gives them something to do. When they do it and get a result, the trust loop starts. In PTL terms, your credibility shows power (you have what they want). Your framework builds trust: they listen, they get a result. And the story of how you learned it builds likeness: they see themselves in where you started. Here's what pairing looks like with my own banks:

  • "I've written over 60,000 tweets" + the 3 Rs = "I've written over 60,000 tweets. Here's how I never run out of ideas: I repost my winners every 90 days, remix them into new formats and repurpose them across every platform."
  • "I've written a newsletter every single week for 5 years" + the 5 Cs = "I've written a newsletter every single week for 5 years. Here are the 5 Cs of writing that people read:"
  • "I've grown my audience to 500,000+ people" + PTL = "I've grown my audience to 500,000+ people. Every post should do at least one of 3 jobs: power, trust or likeness." The template is simple:

"I've [credibility]. Here's the [framework] I use to [result]:" And here's the fun part. The banks multiply. If you have 10 credibility points and 10 frameworks, that's 100 pairings before you ever run out of ideas. Then run each one through the 3 Rs (repost, remix, repurpose) and you'll have content for years. What formats work best for the pairing? The pairing works on any platform. A few formats I come back to on X, LinkedIn, Instagram and YouTube:

  • The how-to: "How I [result] in [timeframe]," then the steps.
  • The big brother (or big sister) post: you talk to someone a few steps behind you. "I've [credibility]. If I were starting over, here's what I'd do."
  • The breakdown: you run something through your framework in public. A landing page through the 6 Ps, a profile through PTL.
  • The client story: someone used your framework, here's what happened.
  • The mistake post: what you got wrong before the framework existed. People don't like people who win all the time.

How does trust turn into customers?

If you do all of this right, you'll feel it. Prospects book calls talking about a problem you wrote about in a post. They reference things you made. Other founders start asking how you're getting so much inbound. My rules for turning trust into business: Give your best stuff away. Sharing your best stuff makes people want to work with you more. Give, give, give, then ask. Promote rarely and wrap it in value. On X, I'd promote about 1 in 15 posts. Every other post should make someone's life a little better. Use one CTA. One CTA, one avatar, one offer. If you give people multiple options, you decrease the likelihood they do anything. Say who it's for, what they need to do, when and how.

So what does the whole system look like?

Here's your trust-building system:

  1. Attention is cheap. Anyone can get it. The asset now is trust.
  2. Trust is belief with evidence. People trust you when they believe listening to you will net them positive results.
  3. Trust gets built in a loop. You tell people to do something, they listen, they get a good result.
  4. Make procedural content, not only declarative. Give people something to DO.
  5. Don't let AI write your insights. It gives you the average, and the average blends in.
  6. Only talk about what you know super well and can prove.
  7. Build your credibility bank (your reasons to listen) and your frameworks bank (what people can go do).
  8. Pair them. Credibility + framework = valuable content. We're in a trust recession. Content is everywhere, and less and less of it gets believed. From here, the people who win are the ones whose advice works when you follow it. Go build your banks. Jay "Earned Secrets" Yang Ps.
