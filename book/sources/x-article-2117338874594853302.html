The Trust Recession: How to Make Content That Builds Authority and Converts

~4,000 words. Save this for later if you don't have 15 minutes :)

Why is it harder to grow a business with content now, when it's never been easier to make it?

AI has made it easier to make more content. You can open a chat window and get a LinkedIn post, a tweet thread and a YouTube script in under a minute. The barrier to entry has never been lower. And when the barrier to entry drops, supply goes up. There's more content going out today than at any point in history, and most of it sounds the same. The asset used to be attention. But now attention is cheap. Anyone can get attention by posting something controversial, hopping on a trend, or filming a skit. Attention isn't the hard part anymore. The true asset now is trust. This essay is for educational creators and business owners who want to use content to grow their business, not to entertain people. If your content gets views but doesn't get you clients, customers or calls, this is probably why. In this essay, I want to explain what trust is, how it gets built, why most content (and almost all AI content) can't build it, and the 2 exercises I'd do today so you can make content only you can make.

What is trust?

Trust is belief with evidence. If you have belief without evidence, that's called faith. When I say someone trusts you, I mean they believe that listening to you will net them positive results. If you can earn people's trust before you sell to them, it'll make everything easier:

  • Leads convert at a higher rate, because they feel like they know, like and trust you. They're already pre-sold.
  • Customers are willing to pay more, buy more often and stay longer.
  • They give you the benefit of the doubt. When anyone can generate content, the people consuming it get more skeptical. They've seen the same "7 lessons from Elon Musk" thread written 100 different ways. We are in a trust recession. There's more content than ever and less reason to believe any of it. That's bad news if you make generic content. It's great news if you don't, because when trust is scarce, the people who have it win by a lot.

So how do you get trust?

You have to tell people to do stuff, they have to listen, and they have to get good results. That's it. If I give you a direction, say "drink more water," and you do it, and you get a positive result (you're not thirsty and you feel more energized), you associate that good result with me. So the next time I tell you to do something, the likelihood you comply goes up. Then you do the next one, it works again, and the association gets stronger. Why does Martha Stewart have so much trust? For pretty much decades, Martha Stewart gave the women in her audience step-by-step directions to get a positive result. She gave them recipes. These women would follow them, and they'd bake something good. Then they'd get compliments from their spouses, neighbors, and kids. They'd associate that good feeling with Martha. So the next time she told them to do something (buy my cookbook, buy my course, whatever), these women listened. Why do you trust your parents? This is also why our parents have a ton of trust with us. For years they told us: drink milk and you'll grow tall. Eat your veggies and you'll be healthy. Don't touch the fire. And for years we listened and got positive results. So we have high trust with our parents. What does this look like with content? Someone read one of my articles, did the PTL framework and got clarity on their brand positioning. I gave a direction (here's a framework, run your brand through it), they listened and got a result, and now that result is tied to me. So the next time I post a framework, they're more likely to try it. So the question to ask yourself is: What step-by-step directions can I give my audience for them to follow and get positive results? Because you want that association in their head. "Hey, listening to this person gives me positive results."

Why doesn't most content build trust?

There are 2 types of knowledge:

  • Declarative knowledge is knowledge ABOUT something. What it is, why it matters.
  • Procedural knowledge is knowledge on HOW to do something. The steps. Most content on the internet is declarative, and not enough is procedural. Let's go back to the trust loop. For trust to get built, I have to tell you to do something, you have to do it, and you have to get a result. Declarative content skips the first step, because there's nothing to do, so there's no result and no association with you. For example: Declarative: "Consistency is the key to growing on LinkedIn." You read it, nod and keep scrolling, and nothing changes. Procedural: "Every Sunday, I block off 2 hours and write 2 weeks of content. I open a Google Doc and write down 6 categories: far past, recent past, current, future, client stories and hypotheticals. For the far past, I scroll back through my photo album (1 year ago today, 2 years ago, 5 years ago). For the recent past, I open my Google Calendar and look at last week's meetings and tasks. Then I write one post for each idea." You can do that this Sunday. And if you do it and you end up with 2 weeks of content, who do you think of? Procedural content makes people DO something, and doing something is how they get a result they can tie back to you. Pro tip: you don't have to cut declarative content. For educational content, I use What / Why / How. The what and the why set it up. The how is the part people can go do, and that's where the trust gets built.

How do you make your content more procedural?

  1. Write the body as What / Why / How.
  • What: the point you're trying to make.
  • Why: why people should care.
  • How: how they implement it. For example: you should drink more water (what). It helps you think more clearly, speak more articulately, makes your skin glow and gives you more energy (why). Before every meal, drink an 8 oz glass of water, which adds up to 24 ounces a day and is more than enough to get started (how).
  1. Make it concrete. Make your writing visualizable, so your reader can picture it. Instead of "we help business owners grow," say "we took a roofing company from 14 leads a month to 70 leads a month." Instead of "post consistently," say "every Sunday, block off 2 hours."
  2. Lower the perceived effort. Perceived effort is how much effort it takes to read your piece AND to implement it. The smaller the first step, the more people take it, and the more people take it, the more people get a result they tie back to you.

Why does AI content make you blend in?

If you use AI to make your content, it will put out shitty, surface-level declarative knowledge. I hate using AI to write, but I love using AI for everything else. The problem is what AI learned from. It learned from the internet, and most of the internet is declarative. So when you ask it for a post on "how to build a personal brand," it gives you the average of every post on building a personal brand. Be authentic. Provide value. Stay consistent. None of that is wrong, but anyone could have written it. This will make you blend in. And when you blend in, you have a weak brand, because nothing you post gives anyone a reason to remember you over the other 1,000 accounts saying the same stuff. If you're a robot spitting out educational information, that's great, but you also sound like AI. People don't only want to learn information. They want to connect with the messenger. This is why the market for content can't be saturated. The messenger changes the message. Listening to an idea from me is different than listening to the same idea from someone else, because I bring my stories, mistakes and results to it. Here's a test I run on everything I write. I call it the Name Test: cover your name and your photo. Could someone tell it was you? If not, it's too generic to post.

So what should you make content about?

Instead of AI's average, you want to use your own deep insights, your own earned secrets and the specific knowledge you picked up from years of doing it and helping other people do it. An earned secret is something you only know because you paid for it. You paid with time, reps, mistakes, money or clients, so you can't Google it. I'll give you an example. I put out a 10,000-word essay the other week, The 2027 Marketing Playbook. It drove a lot of business. People said it was the best article they'd read and that it wasn't AI slop. Wanna know why they liked it? Because it took me 6 years to learn those lessons. The frameworks in that essay came from work I did. The 6 Ps are the process I run before I write anything for a client. The content multiplication system is how I've posted 60,000+ tweets. The why they buy sheet came from transcribing client sales calls and pulling out the words customers used. When you write about others, you build an audience. When you write about yourself, your stories and learnings, you build authority. This is why I'm not a big fan of the "here's what Elon Musk did, and here are 7 lessons for you" posts, because they can get reach, but they don't prove you know anything.

How do you know which directions to give?

Ask your audience. They'll tell you exactly what they're stuck on, and every pain point is a set of directions waiting to be written. 4 ways to collect pain points:

  1. DM new followers. "Hey, saw you followed me. Just want to introduce myself. Why'd you follow me, and what are you interested in learning?" People will tell you.
  2. Run Q&A posts. "I'm free for the next hour, ask me anything about writing on LinkedIn." Be specific about the topic, or people won't know what to ask.
  3. Read your comments. If I post a marathon training plan and someone comments, "This won't work for me, I'm coming back from an injury," that's my next post.
  4. Run free workshops. Get 30 people on a Zoom, have them ask questions, then pull the transcript and list every question that came up. Put them all in one doc. Then turn each pain point into content. Say someone DMs me, "I'm trying to land my first client and I'm struggling." That one pain point turns into:
  • Here are 3 mistakes to fix before you pitch.
  • Here's the exact way I landed my first client.
  • Here's the mistake that cost me my first client, and what I'd do instead.
  • Hot take: you don't need a portfolio to land your first client.
  • 5 signs you're underpriced on your first client. If you collect 50 pain points, that's a few hundred pieces of procedural content, and every one of them is something your audience told you they need.

What happens when you talk about stuff you don't know?

Anyone can pick up a phone and hit record. Don't make content on things you're not an expert in. You'll lose credibility. Think about the trust loop again. If you give someone a direction outside your expertise, they follow it, and it doesn't work, the trust loop flips. Now they associate a bad result with you, and the next time you tell them to do something, they're less likely to listen. Only talk about topics you know super well and have the proof to back up your claims. This is how you win. I'll give you an example from my own business. When I started Publish30 (my 30-day LinkedIn writing cohort), I'd only been writing on LinkedIn for about 7 months. I'd learned a lot about the LinkedIn algorithm, but I was by no means an expert. So I partnered with Kevin Shim, who has over 65,000 followers on LinkedIn and ran a ghostwriting agency. Kevin is the person I lean on for the algorithm questions (What's the best time to post? Should I use this feature? Should I be using pictures?) Before you post something, ask yourself 2 questions:

  1. Do I know this super well? Have I done it myself, more than once?
  2. Do I have the proof? Can I point to a result, mine or a client's? If the answer to either one is no, either go get the reps and the proof, or leave the topic to someone who has them.

How do you find your earned secrets?

So... how do you figure out what you know that's worth sharing? 2 exercises for you to implement right now. Fundamentally, every piece of trust-building content needs 2 ingredients:

  1. A reason to listen to you (your credibility).
  2. Something to go do (your framework). The 2 exercises below build a bank of each.

Exercise 1: Build your credibility bank

Write down every single cool accomplishment you've done that you can use in your content. Every result, milestone, and number. Anything someone else would find impressive. Put it all in one doc. Then pull from it every time you write a hook. Here's part of mine:

  • I've written a newsletter every single week for the past 5 years.
  • I've written over 60,000 tweets.
  • I've grown my audience to 500,000+ people.
  • I scaled my new business to six figures in 8 days.
  • I scaled a second business to six figures in 9 days.
  • I've written 2 best-selling books. Why does this matter? Because credibility is the reason someone listens to you in the first place. In my hook formula, credibility sits on top next to benefit and relevance. It answers the reader's first question: why should I listen to you? If you're stuck, run through these prompts:
  1. Results: What numbers have I hit? Revenue, followers, clients, weight lifted, miles run.
  2. Reps: What have I done a lot of? Posts written, calls taken, ads tested, years in the game.
  3. People: Who have I helped, and what happened for them?
  4. Builds: What have I made? Products, businesses, books, courses, teams.
  5. Firsts and fasts: What did I do younger, faster or with less than people expect? Use the credibility that backs up the topic. If you're writing about newsletters, use the newsletter stat, not the tweet count. Dumping every accomplishment into one hook reads as insecure, and it buries the one number that matters. Lead with your own proof. For years, I leaned on the names of people I'd worked with. It worked up to a point. But at some stage, constant name-dropping gets annoying, and it weakens your brand because it's someone else's proof. These days I reach for my own results first and ask, "What can I, and only I, uniquely say?" Keep adding to it. Every time you get a win, a client result or a nice DM, add it to the bank. In a year, you'll be surprised how long it gets.

Exercise 2: Build your frameworks bank

Next, write down your frameworks bank. These are SOPs, steps, checklists and exercises that you use, or that you give to clients or your team, to get results. A framework is a mental model written down. And a mental model is a mental shortcut to do things easier because you've done it so much. AI can't copy that, because you only find the shortcut after you've done the work enough times to know which steps matter. Here's part of mine:

  • PTL (power, trust, likeness): the 3 ways people get influenced.
  • The 6 Ps of copy thinking (people, positioning, promise, proof, priority, process): what I answer before I write a word for a client.
  • The 5 Cs (clear, concise, concrete, conversational, cadence): how I write.
  • The 3 Rs (repost, remix, repurpose) + the Hall of Fame: how I post a ton on every platform.
  • The story bank: 5 minutes at the end of every day writing down what happened, so I always have stories to write about.
  • The North Star filter: the 2 questions I run every big decision through. What do I want, and what inputs increase the likelihood I get it? If you don't have any frameworks, then you need to create some. Here's how I'd start:
  1. Pick something you do over and over for your clients, your team or yourself. Onboarding a client. Writing a sales page. Running a workout.
  2. Write down every step the next time you do it. Don't clean it up yet.
  3. Cut it down to the steps that move the result. Usually it's 3 to 7.
  4. Give it a name. A name is a handle. People remember PTL, but hardly anyone's going to remember "the process I use for influence."
  5. Hand it to someone and see if they get the result. If they do, you have a framework. If they don't, find the step they got stuck on and fix it.

How do you turn the 2 banks into content?

Once you have those, you can pair credibility with a framework, and boom, now you have a piece of valuable content. Credibility + framework = valuable content. Your credibility gives people a reason to listen, and your framework gives them something to do. When they do it and get a result, the trust loop starts. In PTL terms, your credibility shows power (you have what they want). Your framework builds trust: they listen, they get a result. And the story of how you learned it builds likeness: they see themselves in where you started. Here's what pairing looks like with my own banks:

  • "I've written over 60,000 tweets" + the 3 Rs = "I've written over 60,000 tweets. Here's how I never run out of ideas: I repost my winners every 90 days, remix them into new formats and repurpose them across every platform."
  • "I've written a newsletter every single week for 5 years" + the 5 Cs = "I've written a newsletter every single week for 5 years. Here are the 5 Cs of writing that people read:"
  • "I've grown my audience to 500,000+ people" + PTL = "I've grown my audience to 500,000+ people. Every post should do at least one of 3 jobs: power, trust or likeness." The template is simple:

"I've [credibility]. Here's the [framework] I use to [result]:" And here's the fun part. The banks multiply. If you have 10 credibility points and 10 frameworks, that's 100 pairings before you ever run out of ideas. Then run each one through the 3 Rs (repost, remix, repurpose) and you'll have content for years. What formats work best for the pairing? The pairing works on any platform. A few formats I come back to on X, LinkedIn, Instagram and YouTube:

  • The how-to: "How I [result] in [timeframe]," then the steps.
  • The big brother (or big sister) post: you talk to someone a few steps behind you. "I've [credibility]. If I were starting over, here's what I'd do."
  • The breakdown: you run something through your framework in public. A landing page through the 6 Ps, a profile through PTL.
  • The client story: someone used your framework, here's what happened.
  • The mistake post: what you got wrong before the framework existed. People don't like people who win all the time.

How does trust turn into customers?

If you do all of this right, you'll feel it. Prospects book calls talking about a problem you wrote about in a post. They reference things you made. Other founders start asking how you're getting so much inbound. My rules for turning trust into business: Give your best stuff away. Sharing your best stuff makes people want to work with you more. Give, give, give, then ask. Promote rarely and wrap it in value. On X, I'd promote about 1 in 15 posts. Every other post should make someone's life a little better. Use one CTA. One CTA, one avatar, one offer. If you give people multiple options, you decrease the likelihood they do anything. Say who it's for, what they need to do, when and how.

So what does the whole system look like?

Here's your trust-building system:

  1. Attention is cheap. Anyone can get it. The asset now is trust.
  2. Trust is belief with evidence. People trust you when they believe listening to you will net them positive results.
  3. Trust gets built in a loop. You tell people to do something, they listen, they get a good result.
  4. Make procedural content, not only declarative. Give people something to DO.
  5. Don't let AI write your insights. It gives you the average, and the average blends in.
  6. Only talk about what you know super well and can prove.
  7. Build your credibility bank (your reasons to listen) and your frameworks bank (what people can go do).
  8. Pair them. Credibility + framework = valuable content. We're in a trust recession. Content is everywhere, and less and less of it gets believed. From here, the people who win are the ones whose advice works when you follow it. Go build your banks. Jay "Earned Secrets" Yang Ps.
